Part 1 — Hook: Why This Matters

Every profession has its own language. Doctors have Latin. Lawyers have precedent. Chefs have French. Cybersecurity has a vocabulary that sounds like a foreign language, and if you do not speak it, you cannot do the job.

You will sit in a meeting where someone says “we have a zero-day in the third-party library, the SIEM is throwing false positives, and we need to patch before the next credentialed scan.” You will not understand any of it, and you will be too embarrassed to ask. Then you will make a decision based on half-understood words, and that decision will be wrong.

This lesson fixes that. We are going to learn the 60 most important words in the field, organised into groups so they connect to each other. Each word gets a simple definition, a real example, and a note on why it matters. By the end of this lesson you will be able to read a security report, listen to a security meeting, and understand what people are actually talking about.

The best part: every word in this lesson is a word you will use again. This is not a vocabulary list to memorise and forget. It is the toolbox you will carry for the rest of the course.

Part 2 — Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

Define at least 60 common cybersecurity terms in simple language.
Explain the difference between commonly confused pairs: virus/worm, confidentiality/privacy, vulnerability/exploit, threat/risk.
Use the terms correctly in a sentence.
Connect each term to the lessons you have already taken.
Read a basic security report and identify the key terms.
Part 3 — Prerequisites

You should have finished Lessons 1 through 6. This lesson draws on all of them, and every term connects back to the CIA triad, the kill chain, the assets, the attack surface, and the ecosystem.

Part 4 — Concept from Scratch

Here is the central sentence of this lesson.

Terminology is not a list of words to memorise. It is a set of shared concepts that let you and your team see the same problem from the same angle.

Let me say that again, because it is the difference between a student and a professional.

Terminology is not a list of words to memorise. It is a set of shared concepts that let you and your team see the same problem from the same angle.

When a security team says “we have a vulnerability,” they are not using jargon for its own sake. They are pointing at a specific thing — a weakness in a specific system — so that everyone in the room is looking at the same object. If you do not know the word, you are looking at something else, and the conversation goes nowhere.

This lesson is organised into nine groups. Each group is a cluster of related ideas. Learn the clusters and the individual words will take care of themselves.

Part 5 — How It Works: The Terms, Group by Group
Group 1: Foundations — The Core Concepts

These are the words from Lessons 1 through 5. You have already met them, but here they are in one place, with sharper definitions.

Asset — anything with value whose loss would harm an organisation. Data, systems, people, physical things. From Lesson 3.

Threat — anything that can damage an asset. An attacker, a malware, a natural disaster, a careless employee. A threat is a potential harm. It has not happened yet.

Vulnerability — a weakness in a system that a threat can exploit. A missing patch, a weak password, a misconfigured firewall. A vulnerability is a hole. It is the thing the attacker walks through.

Exploit — a tool or technique that takes advantage of a vulnerability. An exploit is the key that opens the lock. The vulnerability is the weak lock. The exploit is the person who picks it.

Risk — the probability that a threat will exploit a vulnerability and cause harm. Risk = Threat × Vulnerability × Impact. You cannot eliminate risk. You can only reduce it.

Control — anything that reduces risk. A firewall, a policy, a training programme, a backup. Controls are divided into three types: preventive (stop the attack before it happens — a firewall), detective (notice the attack — a log monitor), and corrective (fix the damage — a backup restore).

CIA triad — the three core goals of security: Confidentiality, Integrity, Availability. From Lesson 1.

Confidentiality — keeping secrets secret. Only authorised people see the data.

Integrity — keeping data correct and unbroken. The data has not been tampered with.

Availability — the system is working and accessible when it is needed.

Non-repudiation — the ability to prove that someone did something. A digital signature proves that a message came from a specific person and that they cannot later deny it. This is the fourth pillar, beyond the CIA triad, and it matters in law and finance.

Defence in depth — using multiple layers of security so that if one fails, another stops the attacker. From Lesson 1.

Least privilege — giving each person or system only the access they need, and nothing more. This is the most important principle in access control, and it is the answer to almost every security problem.

Zero trust — the principle of never trusting and always verifying. Assume the attacker may already be inside the network. Verify every request, every time, from every source. This is the modern replacement for the old model of “trust but verify,” which assumed the attacker was outside.

Group 2: Malware — The Weapons

Malware is short for “malicious software.” It is any software designed to harm a system. The different types of malware are distinguished by how they spread and what they do.

Virus — malware that attaches itself to a legitimate file or program and spreads only when that file is opened or shared. A virus needs a host. It cannot spread on its own. The Melissa virus from Lesson 2 was a macro virus that lived inside Word documents.

Worm — malware that spreads on its own through network weaknesses. A worm does not need a host file. It does not need a user to open anything. It finds a vulnerability and exploits it automatically. The Morris Worm from Lesson 2 was the first internet worm. The WannaCry attack from Lesson 2 was a worm.

Trojan — malware that pretends to be something useful. The user installs it voluntarily, believing it is a game, a utility, or a software update. The Trojan is the oldest form of malware, named after the Trojan Horse. It does not spread on its own. It relies on the user’s willingness to install it.

Ransomware — malware that encrypts the victim’s files and demands a ransom to decrypt them. From Lesson 2, WannaCry was the most famous example. Ransomware is now a multi-billion-dollar industry.

Spyware — malware that secretly collects information about the user: keystrokes, passwords, messages, browsing history. It is invisible and persistent.

Adware — malware that displays unwanted advertisements. It is usually less harmful than spyware, but it degrades performance and can be a delivery vehicle for worse malware.

Rootkit — malware that hides itself from the operating system and security tools. It operates at the deepest level of the system, often by modifying the kernel. A rootkit is very difficult to detect and remove.

Botnet — a network of infected devices controlled remotely by an attacker. The devices are called “bots” or “zombies.” The botnet is used for DDoS attacks, spam, stealing data, or hosting malicious content. The Mirai botnet from Lesson 2 was built from internet-connected cameras and routers.

Keylogger — a specific type of spyware that records every keystroke the user makes. It can capture passwords, credit card numbers, and private messages.

Fileless malware — malware that does not write files to disk. It runs entirely in memory, which makes it invisible to traditional antivirus software. It is a growing trend, because it is harder to detect.

Group 3: Attacks — How the Enemy Strikes

These are the specific methods attackers use. Each one has a name, a technique, and a defence.

Phishing — a fake email or message designed to trick the recipient into clicking a malicious link, opening a malicious attachment, or revealing a password. It is the most common attack method, and it works because it exploits trust and urgency. From Lesson 2, the Melissa virus and the Target breach both started with phishing.

Spear phishing — a phishing attack targeted at a specific person or organisation. It is personalised. The attacker researches the victim and makes the email look legitimate. Whaling is spear phishing aimed at executives.

Smishing — phishing by SMS text message. Vishing — phishing by voice call. These are growing rapidly because people are more likely to answer a phone call or open a text than to open an email attachment.

DDoS — Distributed Denial of Service. An attack that floods a website or service with traffic from thousands of devices until it cannot respond to legitimate users. From Lesson 2, the Dyn attack in 2016 was a DDoS. The devices used were infected cameras and routers.

Man-in-the-Middle (MitM) — an attack where the attacker intercepts the communication between two parties without either knowing. The attacker can read, modify, or inject messages. This is why HTTPS exists — it prevents MitM attacks on the web.

SQL injection — an attack where the attacker inserts malicious SQL code into a web form or URL parameter. If the application does not sanitise input, the attacker can read, modify, or delete the database. This is one of the most common web vulnerabilities, and it has been in the OWASP Top Ten for two decades.

Cross-Site Scripting (XSS) — an attack where the attacker injects malicious JavaScript into a web page that other users then execute in their browsers. It is used to steal session cookies, redirect users to malicious sites, or deface the page.

Cross-Site Request Forgery (CSRF) — an attack where the attacker tricks a user’s browser into performing an action the user did not intend, such as transferring money or changing an email address. The attack works because the website trusts the browser’s cookies.

Buffer overflow — a programming error where a program writes more data into a memory space than it can hold, overwriting adjacent memory. This is one of the oldest and most dangerous vulnerabilities. The SQL Slammer worm from Lesson 2 exploited a buffer overflow.

Social engineering — using psychology to trick people into giving away information or access, rather than using technical exploits. It is the technique behind every phishing attack. It works because it exploits human nature: trust, fear, greed, urgency, and helpfulness.

Insider threat — an attack or policy violation carried out by someone with legitimate access to the organisation. It can be malicious (a disgruntled employee stealing data) or accidental (an employee emailing sensitive data to the wrong address).

Supply chain attack — an attack that targets a vendor or software provider in order to reach their customers indirectly. From Lesson 2, the SolarWinds attack was the most sophisticated supply chain attack in history.

Rogue access point — an unauthorized wireless access point set up on a corporate network. It can be used to intercept traffic or provide a backdoor into the network.

Password spraying — an attack where the attacker tries a small number of common passwords against many accounts, instead of trying many passwords against one account. This bypasses account lockout policies.

Group 4: Defence — How We Protect Ourselves

Every attack has a defence. These are the tools and techniques.

Firewall — a network security device that controls incoming and outgoing traffic based on a set of rules. It is the gatekeeper. It allows good traffic through and blocks bad traffic. A firewall is a preventive control.

IDS / IPS — Intrusion Detection System and Intrusion Prevention System. An IDS monitors network traffic and alerts when it detects suspicious activity. An IPS does the same thing, but it also blocks the traffic automatically. The IDS is a detective control. The IPS is both detective and preventive.

Antivirus / Anti-malware — software that scans files and processes for known malware signatures and blocks them. It is a preventive control. It is effective against known malware, but it is weak against new or fileless malware.

Encryption — the process of converting data into a form that cannot be read without a key. It protects data at rest (on a disk) and in transit (over a network). Encryption is the most important privacy control, and it is the reason your credit card transactions are safe.

Multi-factor authentication (MFA) — requiring two or more forms of verification to prove identity. Something you know (a password), something you have (a phone or token), and something you are (a fingerprint). MFA is the single most effective security control available. It stops almost all phishing and password theft.

VPN — Virtual Private Network. A technology that creates an encrypted tunnel between your device and a remote network, hiding your traffic from anyone on the internet. It is essential for remote workers and for protecting public Wi-Fi use.

SIEM — Security Information and Event Management. A system that collects and analyses log data from across the organisation. It correlates events to detect attacks, generates alerts, and provides a searchable record for investigation. SIEM is the detective control that caught the SolarWinds attack.

SOAR — Security Orchestration, Automation, and Response. A system that automates the response to security alerts. When the SIEM detects an attack, the SOAR can automatically isolate the affected system, block the IP address, and notify the team. It reduces the time between detection and response.

Patch — a software update that fixes a known vulnerability. Patching is the most effective security control there is, and it is also the most neglected. From Lesson 2, the Morris Worm, SQL Slammer, and WannaCry all exploited vulnerabilities for which patches existed.

Honeypot — a decoy system designed to look like a valuable target, used to detect and study attackers. The attacker wastes time on the honeypot while the real systems are safe.

Endpoint protection — security software installed on individual devices: laptops, phones, servers. It includes antivirus, firewall, and behaviour monitoring. Modern endpoint protection is cloud-based and uses artificial intelligence to detect unknown threats.

Web application firewall (WAF) — a firewall specifically designed for web applications. It filters and monitors HTTP traffic between a web application and the internet. It blocks common web attacks like SQL injection and XSS.

Segmentation — dividing a network into zones so that a breach in one zone cannot spread to others. From Lesson 4, segmentation is one of the three principles for reducing the attack surface.

Air gap — physically disconnecting a system from the internet or any untrusted network. It is the strongest possible isolation, used for the most sensitive systems. From Lesson 2, Stuxnet showed that even air-gapped systems can be compromised.

Group 5: Access Control — Who Can Do What

Access control is the system that decides who can see and use what.

Authentication — proving who you are. You do this with something you know (a password), something you have (a phone), or something you are (a fingerprint).

Authorization — deciding what you are allowed to do after you are authenticated. Authentication answers “who are you?” Authorization answers “what can you do?”

Account — a unique identity in a system. Each person should have exactly one account, and that account should be tied to that person. Shared accounts are a security disaster, because nobody knows who did what.

Password — the most common form of authentication. It is weak on its own, but strong when combined with MFA. Passwords should be long, unique, and stored in a password manager.

Password manager — a tool that generates and stores unique passwords for every account. It is the answer to password reuse, which is the single most common security mistake users make.

Single Sign-On (SSO) — a system that lets a user log in once and access multiple applications without logging in again. It improves security by reducing the number of passwords, but it also creates a single point of failure.

Federation — a system that lets authentication credentials from one organisation be trusted by another. When you log in with “Sign in with Google,” you are using federation. Google is the identity provider. The website you are logging into is the relying party.

RBAC — Role-Based Access Control. Access is granted based on a person’s role in the organisation. A marketing person can access marketing data. A finance person can access finance data. RBAC is the standard model for large organisations.

ABAC — Attribute-Based Access Control. Access is granted based on attributes: who the user is, what they are doing, where they are, and what time it is. It is more flexible than RBAC and is used for complex environments.

Privileged account — an account with elevated rights: administrator, root, superuser. These accounts are the most valuable targets, because they can do anything. They should be used sparingly, monitored closely, and never shared.

Session — a period of ongoing interaction between a user and a system. Sessions are identified by a session token. Session fixation and session hijacking are attacks that steal or manipulate these tokens.

Just-in-time access — granting access only when it is needed, for the shortest possible time, and removing it immediately afterward. This is the modern implementation of least privilege.

Group 6: Assessment — Testing the Defences

You cannot manage what you cannot measure. These are the tools for measuring security.

Vulnerability scan — an automated test that looks for known vulnerabilities in a system. It is like a medical checkup. It tells you what is wrong, but it does not tell you how bad it is.

Penetration test (pentest) — a simulated attack on a system carried out by a security professional. It is like a stress test. It tries to break in the way a real attacker would, and it reports what it found. A pentest is more thorough and more valuable than a vulnerability scan.

Red team / Blue team — a red team simulates attackers. A blue team defends. A purple team combines both, so that the defenders learn from the attackers and the attackers learn from the defenders. This is the most realistic form of security testing.

Bug bounty — a programme that pays independent researchers for finding and reporting vulnerabilities. It is a way of hiring thousands of testers, because the best researchers will find problems your internal team cannot.

Risk assessment — the process of identifying assets, threats, and vulnerabilities, and calculating the risk. It is the foundation of every security programme. From Lesson 3, it starts with the asset inventory.

Security audit — a formal review of an organisation’s security controls against a standard. The standard might be ISO 27001, PCI DSS, or a regulatory requirement. An audit produces a report that can be shown to customers, regulators, and insurers.

SOC — Security Operations Center. The room where the security team works. It is also the team itself: the people who monitor, detect, and respond to incidents 24 hours a day.

Threat hunting — the proactive search for attackers who have already bypassed defences. Instead of waiting for an alert, the hunter actively looks for signs of compromise. This is the most advanced form of detection.

Group 7: Operations — Running the Programme

These are the words of the daily work.

Incident — a security event that has actually caused harm or is likely to. A port scan is an event. A successful breach is an incident.

Incident response — the process of handling an incident. The standard model is: prepare, detect, contain, eradicate, recover, and learn. Each phase has specific actions.

Containment — stopping the attack from spreading. This is the most urgent phase of incident response. You isolate the affected systems before the attacker can move further.

Eradication — removing the attacker’s tools and access from the system. This is harder than it sounds, because attackers install multiple backdoors.

Recovery — restoring systems to normal operation. This is where backups are used, and why backups must be tested.

Forensics — the science of analysing digital evidence after an incident. It involves recovering deleted files, analysing logs, and reconstructing what the attacker did. Forensic evidence must be handled carefully to be admissible in court.

Log — a record of an event. Logs are the most important data in security, because you cannot investigate what you did not record. From Lesson 2, every major breach was discovered by looking at logs.

SIEM — see Group 4. Logs are the fuel that SIEMs consume.

Business continuity — the ability of an organisation to continue operating during a disruption. It is the planning that happens before the incident.

Disaster recovery — the process of recovering after a disaster. It is the execution of the business continuity plan.

Change management — the process of approving and documenting changes to systems. It is a security control, because an unapproved change is a potential vulnerability.

Group 8: Compliance and Law — The Rules

Security is not just technical. It is also legal.

Compliance — meeting the requirements of a law, regulation, or standard. Compliance is necessary but not sufficient. A company can be compliant and still be breached.

GDPR — the General Data Protection Regulation, the European Union’s privacy law. It requires consent for data collection, gives individuals the right to access and delete their data, and imposes fines of up to 4% of global revenue.

PCI DSS — the Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard. A set of security requirements for any organisation that handles credit card data. It is enforced by the card brands, not by the government.

ISO 27001 — an international standard for information security management systems. It provides a framework for building a security programme. Certification to ISO 27001 is a common requirement in contracts.

DPDP Act 2023 — India’s Digital Personal Data Protection Act. It establishes the rules for how personal data is collected, processed, and protected in India.

CERT-In — India’s Computer Emergency Response Team. It issues security advisories, coordinates incident response, and requires organisations to report breaches within six hours.

IT Act 2000 — India’s Information Technology Act. It defines cybercrime, establishes penalties, and provides the legal framework for electronic contracts and signatures.

Data breach — a security incident in which unauthorised access to personal or sensitive data occurs. Most countries now require data breaches to be reported to regulators and affected individuals.

Privacy policy — a document that explains what data an organisation collects, why, how it is used, and who it is shared with. It is a legal requirement under most privacy laws.

Consent — informed agreement to the collection and use of personal data. Consent must be specific, informed, and freely given. Pre-ticked boxes are not valid consent.

Group 9: Infrastructure — The Building Blocks

These are the components of the internet and networks.

DNS — the Domain Name System. It is the phonebook of the internet. When you type github.com, your computer asks the DNS “what is the IP address?” and the DNS answers. The Dyn attack in Lesson 2 was a DDoS against DNS.

CDN — Content Delivery Network. A network of servers that copies website content to servers around the world, so that users get it from a nearby location. It improves speed and absorbs DDoS attacks.

Cloud — computing services delivered over the internet instead of on local servers. It is convenient, it is scalable, and it is a major source of misconfiguration errors.

API — Application Programming Interface. A set of rules that lets two software applications talk to each other. APIs are everywhere, and they are a major attack surface.

SSL / TLS — the protocols that encrypt communication on the web. HTTPS is HTTP over TLS. TLS prevents eavesdropping and man-in-the-middle attacks.

Port — a number that identifies a specific service on a computer. Port 80 is HTTP. Port 443 is HTTPS. Port 22 is SSH. An open port is a potential attack vector.

Protocol — a set of rules that governs how devices communicate on a network. TCP, UDP, HTTP, and DNS are all protocols.

Subnet — a subdivision of a network. Subnetting is the technique of dividing a network into smaller, more manageable pieces. It is fundamental to network segmentation.

Router — a device that forwards packets between networks. It is the traffic controller of the internet. A home router is the boundary between your home network and the internet.

Switch — a device that connects devices within a single network. It is more intelligent than a hub, because it sends data only to the device it is addressed to.

Load balancer — a device that distributes incoming traffic across multiple servers. It prevents any single server from being overwhelmed. It is essential for high-traffic websites.

Part 6 — The Confused Pairs: Words That Sound Alike but Mean Different Things

Now let us spend a moment on the pairs that cause the most trouble. If you can separate these, your professional vocabulary is solid.

Virus vs. Worm. A virus needs a host and a user action to spread. A worm spreads on its own through the network. A virus is a passenger. A worm is a self-driving car.

Threat vs. Risk. A threat is anything that can cause harm. Risk is the probability that the harm will actually happen. A tiger is a threat. The risk of being bitten depends on whether you are in the same room as the tiger.

Vulnerability vs. Exploit. A vulnerability is a weakness. An exploit is the tool that takes advantage of the weakness. A broken lock is the vulnerability. The key that opens it is the exploit.

Confidentiality vs. Privacy. Confidentiality is about keeping data secret from everyone who is not authorised. Privacy is about controlling your own data. Confidentiality is a property of the data. Privacy is a right of the person.

Authentication vs. Authorization. Authentication is proving who you are. Authorization is deciding what you can do. Authentication is the ID check at the door. Authorization is the key to the room inside.

IDS vs. IPS. An IDS detects and alerts. An IPS detects, alerts, and blocks. An IDS is a security camera. An IPS is a security camera with a gun.

Antivirus vs. Anti-malware. These terms are often used interchangeably, but there is a difference. Antivirus focuses on known malware signatures. Anti-malware uses behaviour analysis to catch unknown threats. Modern products do both.

Compliance vs. Security. Compliance is meeting the rules. Security is being safe. A company can be compliant and still be breached. A company can be secure and still fail an audit. They are related but not the same.

DDoS vs. DoS. A DoS (Denial of Service) comes from one source. A DDoS (Distributed DoS) comes from many sources. The difference is scale, and it is the difference between a protest and an army.

Phishing vs. Spear phishing. Phishing is sent to many people and is generic. Spear phishing is sent to one person and is personalised. The difference is effort, and spear phishing is much more dangerous.

Part 7 — Attack and Defence View

Let us now look at these terms from the perspective of the attacker and the defender, because the same word means different things depending on which side you are on.

A vulnerability is a hole to the attacker and a responsibility to the defender. The attacker looks for holes. The defender closes them.

A log is a record to the defender and a liability to the attacker. The defender keeps logs to catch attackers. The attacker deletes logs to avoid being caught.

A password is a key to the user, a weakness to the defender, and a target to the attacker. The user thinks of it as a formality. The defender knows it is the weakest link. The attacker tries to steal it first.

A patch is a fix to the vendor, a responsibility to the defender, and an obstacle to the attacker. The vendor writes it. The defender installs it. The attacker hopes nobody does.

An MFA prompt is a convenience to the user, a strong control to the defender, and a barrier to the attacker. The user finds it annoying. The defender knows it stops most attacks. The attacker hates it.

This is why terminology matters. If you and your team do not use the same words, you will not be able to coordinate under pressure. When the incident hits, there is no time to explain what you mean. You need shared language, and this lesson is that shared language.

Part 8 — Common Mistakes Beginners Make

Mistake 1: Memorising definitions without understanding the connections. A word is not an island. Every word in this lesson connects to at least three others. Vulnerability connects to threat, risk, exploit, and patch. Encryption connects to confidentiality, TLS, and privacy. Learn the clusters, not the list.

Mistake 2: Using terms loosely. “The firewall was breached” is sloppy. A firewall is a device. An attacker does not breach a firewall. They bypass it, or they exploit a misconfiguration in it. Precision in language is precision in thought, and precision saves lives when the incident hits.

Mistake 3: Confusing related terms. The ten confused pairs in Part 6 are the ones that get people in trouble. If you cannot explain the difference between a threat and a risk, you cannot do a risk assessment. If you cannot explain the difference between a virus and a worm, you cannot write a malware analysis report.

Mistake 4: Treating this as a one-time lesson. New terms appear every year. Cloud security, AI security, and supply chain security each have their own vocabularies. You will learn them when you get there. The method is the same: group them, connect them, and use them.

Mistake 5: Using jargon to sound impressive. This is the worst mistake, because it hides confusion. If you cannot explain a term in simple language, you do not understand it. Every term in this lesson has a simple explanation. If you find yourself using jargon, stop and find the simple version.

Part 9 — Guided Mini-Lab: Use the Terms in a Sentence

Let us now practice. This takes 15 minutes. Do not just read the definitions. Write the sentences.

Step 1: Write five sentences using the core terms.
Use each term once. Make the sentences about your own life, so they stick.

Threat: “The biggest threat to my online accounts is ______.”
Vulnerability: “The biggest vulnerability in my digital life is ______.”
Risk: “The risk I am most worried about is ______.”
Control: “The best security control I use is ______.”
Incident: “If an incident happened, the first thing I would do is ______.”

Step 2: Write three sentences distinguishing confused pairs.

“The difference between a virus and a worm is ______.”
“The difference between a threat and a risk is ______.”
“The difference between authentication and authorization is ______.”

Step 3: Write a paragraph describing a security incident.
Use at least eight terms from this lesson. For example: “An attacker used a phishing email to steal an employee’s credentials. The employee fell for a social engineering attack. The attacker used the credentials to gain unauthorised access to the database. The SIEM detected the unusual login and generated an alert. The security team contained the incident by isolating the affected server. They eradicated the attacker’s access and recovered from backups. The incident was documented in the incident report.”

Part 10 — Why This Matters for the Rest of Your Life

Let me say something plainly.

You are going to learn about firewalls, encryption, penetration testing, and incident response. It is a lot of technical detail, and it is easy to think that the vocabulary is just decoration. It is not.

The vocabulary is the operating system of the profession. Without it, you cannot read the documentation, you cannot follow the incident calls, you cannot write the reports, and you cannot teach the next person. Every term in this lesson is a tool in your toolbox, and you will use almost all of them before you finish this course.

You are learning to speak the language. That is what this lesson is. The lessons before it taught you what to think. This lesson teaches you how to say it.

Part 11 — Summary and Glossary

Here is what we covered.

This lesson introduced 60+ terms organised into nine groups: foundations (asset, threat, vulnerability, exploit, risk, control, CIA triad, defence in depth, least privilege, zero trust), malware (virus, worm, Trojan, ransomware, spyware, adware, rootkit, botnet, keylogger, fileless malware), attacks (phishing, spear phishing, smishing, vishing, DDoS, MitM, SQL injection, XSS, CSRF, buffer overflow, social engineering, insider threat, supply chain attack, rogue access point, password spraying), defence (firewall, IDS/IPS, antivirus, encryption, MFA, VPN, SIEM, SOAR, patch, honeypot, endpoint protection, WAF, segmentation, air gap), access control (authentication, authorization, account, password, password manager, SSO, federation, RBAC, ABAC, privileged account, session, just-in-time access), assessment (vulnerability scan, pentest, red/blue/purple team, bug bounty, risk assessment, security audit, SOC, threat hunting), operations (incident, incident response, containment, eradication, recovery, forensics, log, business continuity, disaster recovery, change management), compliance and law (compliance, GDPR, PCI DSS, ISO 27001, DPDP Act 2023, CERT-In, IT Act 2000, data breach, privacy policy, consent), and infrastructure (DNS, CDN, cloud, API, SSL/TLS, port, protocol, subnet, router, switch, load balancer).

The ten confused pairs are virus/worm, threat/risk, vulnerability/exploit, confidentiality/privacy, authentication/authorization, IDS/IPS, antivirus/anti-malware, compliance/security, DDoS/DoS, and phishing/spear phishing.

Glossary of 12 key terms (for review):

Vulnerability — a weakness in a system that can be exploited.
Exploit — a tool or technique that takes advantage of a vulnerability.
Risk — the probability that a threat will exploit a vulnerability and cause harm.
Malware — any software designed to harm a system.
Phishing — a fake message designed to trick the recipient into revealing information.
MFA — multi-factor authentication; requiring two or more forms of verification.
SIEM — Security Information and Event Management; a system for collecting and analysing logs.
Least privilege — giving each user only the access they need.
Zero trust — never trusting and always verifying.
Penetration test — a simulated attack carried out by a security professional.
Incident response — the process of handling a security incident.
Compliance — meeting the requirements of a law, regulation, or standard.
