Lesson 1: Why Cryptography? Symmetric vs Asymmetric Encryption
Part 1 — Hook: The Problem of the Open Channel

Imagine you are standing in a crowded marketplace, shouting a secret message to your friend standing on the other side. Everyone between you can hear every word. If the message is “Meet me at the cafe at noon,” the risk is low. If the message is “The vault combination is 12-45-33,” the risk is catastrophic.

This is the exact problem the internet solves every millisecond. When you log into your bank, send an email, or visit a website, your data travels across a public, shared infrastructure—routers, switches, cables, and servers owned by dozens of different companies and governments. Every packet you send passes through devices you do not control, operated by people you do not know.

Without protection, anyone with access to that infrastructure—your ISP, a hacker on public Wi-Fi, a malicious router operator, a government agency—can read, modify, inject, or block your data. They can steal your passwords, alter your transactions, impersonate you, or spy on your communications.

Cryptography is the science of communicating securely over an insecure channel. It transforms readable data (plaintext) into unreadable gibberish (ciphertext) using mathematical algorithms and secret keys, ensuring that only the intended recipient can reverse the process. It is the only thing that makes privacy, trust, and security possible on the internet.

Without cryptography, there is no HTTPS, no secure email, no digital signatures, no cryptocurrency, no VPN, no secure messaging, and no modern economy. Every online transaction, every login, every private message relies on the mathematical guarantees we will explore in this lesson.

Part 4 — Concept from Scratch: The Four Pillars of Cryptography

Before we dive into algorithms, we must understand why we use cryptography. It solves four distinct problems. Every cryptographic tool you will ever use—whether it is AES, RSA, TLS, or a digital signature—serves one or more of these four pillars.

              THE FOUR PILLARS OF CRYPTOGRAPHY
┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│                                                                 │
│   CONFIDENTIALITY          INTEGRITY                            │
│   (Secrecy)                (Trustworthiness)                    │
│   "Only authorized eyes     "Data has not been                  │
│    can read this."           altered or corrupted."            │
│   Tool: Encryption           Tool: Hashing / MAC                │
│                                                                 │
│   AUTHENTICATION           NON-REPUDIATION                      │
│   (Identity Verification)  (Undeniable Proof)                   │
│   "You are who you          "You cannot deny                   │
│    say you are."             you did this."                     │
│   Tool: MAC / Signatures   Tool: Digital Signatures             │
│                                                                 │
└─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
1. Confidentiality (Secrecy)

Goal: Ensure that information is accessible only to those authorized to have access.
Mechanism: Encryption. Transforming plaintext into ciphertext using an algorithm and a key. Without the key, the ciphertext reveals nothing about the plaintext.
Example: HTTPS encrypts your credit card number so that the coffee shop Wi-Fi router cannot read it.

2. Integrity (Trustworthiness)

Goal: Ensure that information has not been altered, deleted, or inserted by unauthorized parties.
Mechanism: Hashing and Message Authentication Codes (MACs). A hash function creates a unique “fingerprint” of the data. A MAC uses a secret key to prove the data has not been tampered with.
Example: When you download a software update, the OS checks the hash to ensure the file was not corrupted or replaced with malware during download.

3. Authentication (Identity Verification)

Goal: Verify the identity of the communicating parties.
Mechanism: Message Authentication Codes (MACs), Digital Signatures, Certificates. Proves the message came from the claimed sender and was not forged.
Example: When you connect to bank.com, the server presents a TLS certificate proving it is the real bank, not a phishing site.

4. Non-Repudiation (Undeniable Proof)

Goal: Prevent a party from denying they performed an action (e.g., sending a message, signing a contract).
Mechanism: Digital Signatures. Uses asymmetric cryptography to bind a specific identity to a message in a way that cannot be forged or denied.
Example: You sign a digital contract with your private key. You cannot later claim “I never signed that” because only you possess the private key.

Part 5 — Symmetric Encryption: The Shared Secret

Symmetric encryption is the oldest and simplest form of encryption. It uses one single key for both encryption and decryption. Both the sender and the receiver must possess the exact same secret key.

How It Works: The Mathematical Box

Imagine a magical steel box with a single lock. You put your diary (plaintext) inside, lock it with a key, and send the box to your friend. Your friend uses the exact same key to unlock the box and read the diary. If the key is stolen, the thief can open the box. If the key is lost, the diary is lost forever.

Algorithm: Ciphertext = Encrypt(Key, Plaintext)
Inverse: Plaintext = Decrypt(Key, Ciphertext)

The Critical Challenge: Key Distribution

The fundamental weakness of symmetric encryption is how do you get the key to your friend securely? If you send the key over the same insecure channel, an attacker can intercept it and decrypt all future messages. If you meet in person to exchange the key, you lose the ability to communicate globally at scale. This is the “Key Distribution Problem” that plagued cryptography for millennia.

Major Symmetric Algorithms
Algorithm	Key Size	Block Size	Status	Notes
AES (Advanced Encryption Standard)	128, 192, 256 bits	128 bits	Gold Standard	Mandated by governments worldwide. Hardware-accelerated on all modern CPUs.
ChaCha20	256 bits	Stream (512 bits)	Modern Standard	Faster than AES in software without hardware acceleration. Used in TLS 1.3, WireGuard.
3DES (Triple DES)	168 bits (effective 112)	64 bits	Deprecated	Slow, small block size (birthday attacks). Retired by NIST in 2023.
Blowfish / Twofish	Variable	64 / 128 bits	Legacy	Superseded by AES.
Modes of Operation: ECB vs CBC vs GCM

A block cipher encrypts fixed-size blocks (e.g., 128 bits). How you chain these blocks together is the mode of operation. Choosing the wrong mode is a catastrophic failure.

ECB (Electronic Codebook) — NEVER USE.
Each block is encrypted independently. Identical plaintext blocks produce identical ciphertext blocks.
Failure: Patterns in the plaintext (e.g., a bitmap image) remain visible in the ciphertext. The famous “Linux Penguin in ECB” image proves this visually.
CBC (Cipher Block Chaining) — Legacy.
Each plaintext block is XORed with the previous ciphertext block before encryption. Requires a random Initialization Vector (IV).
Weakness: No integrity protection. Vulnerable to padding oracle attacks (e.g., POODLE). Not parallelizable.
GCM (Galois/Counter Mode) — THE MODERN STANDARD.
Turns a block cipher into a stream cipher. Provides Authenticated Encryption with Associated Data (AEAD).
Features: Confidentiality + Integrity + Authenticity in one pass. Parallelizable. Hardware-accelerated.
Requirement: Never reuse a (Key, Nonce) pair. Ever. Nonce reuse destroys confidentiality and integrity.
Key Sizes and Security Margins
AES-128: 128-bit security margin. Sufficient for most commercial data until quantum computers arrive.
AES-256: 256-bit security margin. Required for TOP SECRET government data. Slightly slower (14 rounds vs 10).
ChaCha20: 256-bit key. Designed to be fast in software without AES-NI hardware instructions.
Part 6 — Asymmetric Encryption: The Public/Private Key Pair

Asymmetric encryption (Public Key Cryptography) solves the Key Distribution Problem by using two mathematically linked keys:

Public Key: Can be shared with the world. Anyone can use it to encrypt data or verify a signature.
Private Key: Must be kept secret. Only the owner can use it to decrypt data or create a signature.
              ASYMMETRIC ENCRYPTION FLOW
┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│                                                                 │
│   SENDER                              RECEIVER                  │
│                                                                 │
│   [ Plaintext ]                                          [ Plaintext ]
│        │                                                   ▲     │
│        │                                                   │     │
│        ▼                                                   │     │
│   Encrypt with                                         Decrypt with    │
│   RECEIVER'S PUBLIC KEY                             RECEIVER'S       │
│   (Anyone can do this)                              PRIVATE KEY      │
│        │                                                   │     │
│        ▼                                                   │     │
│   [ Ciphertext ]  ──────(Insecure Channel)──────>  [ Ciphertext ]    │
│                                                                 │
│   ONLY the receiver's PRIVATE KEY can decrypt.                  │
│   The PUBLIC KEY is useless for decryption.                     │
│                                                                 │
└─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
The Mathematical Foundation: Trapdoor Functions

Asymmetric cryptography relies on mathematical problems that are easy to compute in one direction but infeasible to reverse without a secret “trapdoor.”

Integer Factorization (RSA): Easy to multiply two large primes. Infeasible to factor the product back into the primes.
Discrete Logarithm (Diffie-Hellman, DSA): Easy to compute g^x mod p. Infeasible to find x given g, p, and the result.
Elliptic Curve Discrete Logarithm (ECC/ECDSA): Same logic on elliptic curves. Much smaller keys for equivalent security.
Major Asymmetric Algorithms
Algorithm	Basis	Key Size (Equivalent to AES-128)	Primary Use	Status
RSA	Integer Factorization	3072 bits	Encryption, Signatures, Key Exchange	Legacy standard. Slow. Large keys.
ECC (Elliptic Curve Cryptography)	Elliptic Curve DLP	256 bits	Signatures (ECDSA), Key Exchange (ECDH)	Modern Standard. Small keys, fast.
Ed25519 / X25519	Curve25519	256 bits	Signatures (Ed25519), Key Exchange (X25519)	Gold Standard. Constant-time, side-channel resistant.
Diffie-Hellman (DH)	Discrete Logarithm	3072 bits	Key Exchange only	Legacy. Requires large primes.
Post-Quantum (Kyber, Dilithium)	Lattice / Hash	Variable	KEM / Signatures	Future Standard. NIST PQC winners.
The RSA Trap: Textbook RSA is Insecure

Raw RSA (ciphertext = plaintext^e mod n) is deterministic and malleable.

Deterministic: Same plaintext always produces same ciphertext. Attacker learns if you sent “Yes” or “No”.
Malleable: Attacker can modify ciphertext to change the underlying plaintext predictably.
Solution: Padding Schemes (OAEP). RSA-OAEP adds randomness and structure, making it probabilistic and non-malleable. Always use RSA-OAEP for encryption, RSA-PSS for signatures.
Elliptic Curve Cryptography (ECC): The Modern Standard

ECC provides the same security as RSA with dramatically smaller keys.

AES-128 security ≈ RSA-3072 ≈ ECC-256.
AES-256 security ≈ RSA-15360 ≈ ECC-384.
Smaller keys = faster computation, less bandwidth, less storage, lower CPU/memory usage.
Standard Curves: NIST P-256, P-384 (standardized, widely supported) vs Curve25519 / Curve448 (modern, safer implementation, constant-time).
Use X25519 for Key Exchange (ECDH). Use Ed25519 for Signatures.
Part 7 — The Hybrid Approach: Best of Both Worlds

Symmetric encryption is fast (GB/s) but has a key distribution problem.
Asymmetric encryption solves key distribution but is slow (KB/s, thousands of times slower).

The Solution: Hybrid Encryption (Envelope Encryption).

This is how TLS (HTTPS), PGP, Signal, Age, and almost all modern protocols work.

              HYBRID ENCRYPTION (ENVELOPE ENCRYPTION)
┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│                                                                 │
│   SENDER                                                          │
│   ┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐   │
│   │ 1. Generate Random Symmetric Session Key (K_session)     │   │
│   │    (256 bits, ephemeral, used once)                      │   │
│   └─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘   │
│                          │                                       │
│                          ▼                                       │
│   ┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐   │
│   │ 2. Encrypt Large Data with K_session (AES-256-GCM)      │   │
│   │    Fast! Parallelizable! Authenticated!                  │   │
│   └─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘   │
│                          │                                       │
│                          ▼                                       │
│   ┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐   │
│   │ 3. Encrypt K_session with Receiver's Public Key (RSA-OAEP│   │
│   │    or ECDH/X25519)                                       │   │
│   │    Small! Only 32-48 bytes encrypted asymmetrically.    │   │
│   └─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘   │
│                          │                                       │
│                          ▼                                       │
│   ┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐   │
│   │ 4. Send: [ Encrypted Session Key ] + [ Encrypted Data ]  │   │
│   └─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘   │
│                                                                 │
│   RECEIVER                                                      │
│   1. Decrypt Session Key with Private Key (Slow, once)         │
│   2. Decrypt Data with Session Key (Fast, bulk)                │
│                                                                 │
└─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

Why this is perfect:

Speed: 99.9% of the work is fast symmetric encryption.
Key Distribution: The symmetric key is safely transmitted via asymmetric encryption.
Forward Secrecy: If you use Ephemeral Diffie-Hellman (DHE/ECDHE) to derive the session key instead of encrypting it directly, compromising the long-term private key does not allow decryption of past sessions. This is Perfect Forward Secrecy (PFS). Mandatory in TLS 1.3.
Part 8 — Digital Signatures: Authenticity and Non-Repudiation

Asymmetric cryptography enables Digital Signatures, which provide Authentication, Integrity, and Non-Repudiation.

              DIGITAL SIGNATURE FLOW
┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│                                                                 │
│   SIGNER                                                         │
│   ┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐   │
│   │ 1. Hash the Message (SHA-256) -> Digest (32 bytes)       │   │
│   └─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘   │
│                          │                                       │
│                          ▼                                       │
│   ┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐   │
│   │ 2. Encrypt Digest with SIGNER'S PRIVATE KEY -> Signature │   │
│   └─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘   │
│                          │                                       │
│                          ▼                                       │
│   Send: [ Message ] + [ Signature ]                              │
│                                                                 │
│   VERIFIER                                                       │
│   ┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐   │
│   │ 1. Hash the Received Message -> Digest'                  │   │
│   │ 2. Decrypt Signature with SIGNER'S PUBLIC KEY -> Digest  │   │
│   │ 3. Compare Digest == Digest' ?                           │   │
│   │    YES -> Valid Signature (Authentic, Intact, Non-Repudiable)│
│   │    NO  -> Tampered or Forged                              │   │
│   └─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘   │
│                                                                 │
└─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

Key Properties:

Authenticity: Only the holder of the private key could have created the signature.
Integrity: Changing even one bit of the message changes the hash, invalidating the signature.
Non-Repudiation: The signer cannot deny signing because only they possess the private key.

Algorithms:

RSA-PSS (Probabilistic Signature Scheme): Modern RSA padding for signatures. Probabilistic (random salt).
ECDSA (Elliptic Curve DSA): Standard for Bitcoin, Ethereum, TLS. Requires good randomness (k). Bad RNG = private key leak (PlayStation 3, Bitcoin wallets).
Ed25519 (EdDSA): Gold Standard. Deterministic (no RNG needed for signing). Fast. Side-channel resistant. Small keys (32 bytes). Use this for all new systems.
Part 9 — Attacker vs Defender Viewpoint
The Attacker’s Exploitation Playbook
ATTACKER CRYPTOGRAPHIC EXPLOITATION PLAYBOOK
 ├─ Break the Implementation, Not the Math ──> Side-channels, Timing, Padding Oracles
 ├─ Steal the Keys ─────────────────────────> Memory scraping, HSM bypass, Social engineering
 ├─ Exploit Protocol Flaws ────────────────> Downgrade attacks, Replay, Reflection
 └─ Abuse Key Management ──────────────────> Weak RNG, Key reuse, Hardcoded keys
Break the Implementation, Not the Math: AES-256 is unbreakable. But a cache-timing attack on AES lookup tables recovers the key in minutes. A padding oracle attack on CBC mode decrypts ciphertext without the key. Constant-time implementations and AEAD (GCM) are mandatory.
Steal the Keys: The strongest encryption is useless if the attacker dumps the key from RAM (Mimikatz), extracts it from an HSM via side-channel, or tricks an admin into pasting it into a chat window.
Protocol Downgrade: Attacker forces TLS 1.2 or SSLv3 to exploit known weaknesses (POODLE, BEAST). TLS 1.3 removes negotiation of weak ciphers.
Random Number Generator (RNG) Failure: If the RNG is predictable (e.g., Debian OpenSSL bug, dual_ec_drbg backdoor), all keys generated are compromised. Use system CSPRNG (/dev/urandom, getrandom()).
The Defender’s Hardening Playbook
Use High-Level Libraries, Never DIY Crypto: Use libsodium, OpenSSL (EVP API), Go crypto/tls, Python cryptography. Never implement AES, RSA, or ECC yourself.
Use Authenticated Encryption (AEAD) Everywhere: AES-256-GCM or ChaCha20-Poly1305. Never use raw AES-CBC, ECB, or unauthenticated modes.
Enforce TLS 1.3 Everywhere: Disable TLS 1.0, 1.1, 1.2. Enforce TLS_AES_256_GCM_SHA384 or TLS_CHACHA20_POLY1305_SHA256.
Use Ed25519 for Signatures, X25519 for Key Exchange: Modern, fast, side-channel resistant, no RNG dependency for signing.
Protect Keys in Hardware: Use HSMs, TPMs, Secure Enclaves, or Cloud KMS (AWS KMS, GCP KMS, Azure Key Vault). Keys never leave the hardware boundary.
Rotate Keys and Enforce Short Lifetimes: Automate certificate rotation (ACME/Let’s Encrypt). Rotate data encryption keys annually.
Part 10 — Common Implementation Anti-Patterns & Pitfalls
Anti-Pattern 1: “Rolling Your Own Crypto”
The Mistake: Developers implement AES, RSA, or a custom protocol because “the library is too slow/big/complex.”
Why it Fails: Side-channel leaks, padding oracle vulnerabilities, IV reuse, RNG misuse. You are not smarter than the collective cryptanalysis of the last 30 years.
Remediation: Use libsodium (high-level crypto_box, crypto_secretbox), age (file encryption), TLS 1.3.
Anti-Pattern 2: ECB Mode or Unauthenticated CBC
The Mistake: Using AES-ECB (pattern leakage) or AES-CBC without a MAC (padding oracle, bit-flipping).
Why it Fails: ECB reveals patterns (images, structured data). CBC without HMAC allows padding oracle attacks (POODLE, Lucky13) and bit-flipping attacks.
Remediation: Always use AEAD: AES-GCM or ChaCha20-Poly1305.
Anti-Pattern 3: Static IV / Nonce Reuse in GCM/ChaCha20
The Mistake: Using a fixed IV (all zeros) or a counter that resets on restart for AES-GCM or ChaCha20-Poly1305.
Why it Fails: Nonce reuse in AEAD is catastrophic. It reveals the XOR of plaintexts and allows full forgery of authentication tags. Game over.
Remediation: Use a 96-bit random nonce for GCM (acceptable collision risk after 2^32 messages) or a 192-bit nonce for XChaCha20-Poly1305 (safe for random nonces). Better: Use a construction that derives nonces from a counter stored in stable storage.
Anti-Pattern 4: Hardcoded Keys / Secrets in Code
The Mistake: const string API_KEY = "sk_live_..." or byte[] AES_KEY = { ... } in source code / config files / Docker images.
Why it Fails: Git history leaks keys. Docker layers leak keys. CI logs leak keys. Insiders steal keys.
Remediation: Never hardcode secrets. Use environment variables, secret managers (Vault, AWS Secrets Manager, Doppler), or cloud KMS.
Anti-Pattern 4: Using RSA for Bulk Encryption
The Mistake: Encrypting a 10MB file directly with RSA-OAEP.
Why it Fails: RSA is ~1000x slower than AES. RSA input size is limited by modulus size (e.g., 3072-bit key = 384 bytes max payload with OAEP). You would have to chunk the file, which is slow and error-prone.
Remediation: Always use Hybrid Encryption (Envelope Encryption).
Part 11 — Guided Mini-Lab: Encrypting and Signing with OpenSSL

In this hands-on lab, we will use the openssl command-line tool (available on Linux, macOS, Windows via WSL/Git Bash) to perform symmetric encryption, asymmetric key generation, hybrid encryption, and digital signing.

Prerequisites
bash
openssl version
# Should output OpenSSL 3.x or 1.1.1+
Exercise 1: Symmetric Encryption with AES-256-GCM
bash
# 1. Generate a random 256-bit key and 96-bit IV (nonce)
openssl rand -hex 32 > key.hex
openssl rand -hex 12 > iv.hex

# 2. Create a test file
echo "This is a secret message for the lab." > plaintext.txt

# 3. Encrypt with AES-256-GCM (AEAD)
# -K: key (hex), -iv: nonce (hex), -aad: optional associated data
openssl enc -aes-256-gcm -K $(cat key.hex) -iv $(cat iv.hex) -in plaintext.txt -out ciphertext.bin

# 4. The output includes the ciphertext + auth tag (last 16 bytes)
# Let's decrypt to verify
openssl enc -d -aes-256-gcm -K $(cat key.hex) -iv $(cat iv.hex) -in ciphertext.bin -out decrypted.txt
cat decrypted.txt
Exercise 2: Asymmetric Key Generation (Ed25519 / X25519)
bash
# Generate Ed25519 Private Key (Signing)
openssl genpkey -algorithm ed25519 -out ed25519_private.pem
openssl pkey -in ed25519_private.pem -pubout -out ed25519_public.pem

# Generate X25519 Private Key (Key Agreement / Encryption)
openssl genpkey -algorithm x25519 -out x25519_private.pem
openssl pkey -in x25519_private.pem -pubout -out x25519_public.pem

# View key details
openssl pkey -in ed25519_private.pem -text -noout
Exercise 3: Digital Signature with Ed25519
bash
# 1. Create a document
echo "Contract: Alice pays Bob 100 USD." > contract.txt

# 2. Sign the document with Ed25519 private key
# openssl dgst -sign private_key -out signature.bin file
openssl dgst -sha256 -sign ed25519_private.pem -out contract.sig contract.txt

# 3. Verify the signature with the public key
openssl dgst -sha256 -verify ed25519_public.pem -signature contract.sig contract.txt
# Output: Verified OK

# 4. Tamper test
echo "Contract: Alice pays Bob 999 USD." > contract_tampered.txt
openssl dgst -sha256 -verify ed25519_public.pem -signature contract.sig contract_tampered.txt
# Output: Verification Failure
Exercise 4: Hybrid Encryption (Envelope Encryption) with OpenSSL

Simulating TLS/AGE/PGP logic manually.

bash
# 1. Receiver generates X25519 key pair (done in Exercise 2)

# 2. Sender generates a random session key (32 bytes = 256 bits)
openssl rand -out session_key.bin 32

# 3. Sender encrypts the large file with AES-256-GCM using session key
openssl rand -hex 12 > iv.hex
openssl enc -aes-256-gcm -K $(xxd -p -c 32 session_key.bin) -iv $(cat iv.hex) -in large_file.pdf -out large_file.enc

# 4. Sender encrypts the session key with Receiver's X25519 Public Key
# OpenSSL CLI doesn't directly support X25519 encryption (ECDH key agreement).
# This is typically done programmatically (ECDH key agreement -> HKDF -> AES).
# For CLI demo, we use RSA-OAEP as a classic hybrid example:
openssl genpkey -algorithm rsa -out rsa_private.pem -pkeyopt rsa_keygen_bits:3072
openssl pkey -in rsa_private.pem -pubout -out rsa_public.pem

# Encrypt session key with RSA-OAEP
openssl pkeyutl -encrypt -in session_key.bin -pubin -inkey rsa_public.pem -pkeyopt rsa_padding_mode:oaep -pkeyopt rsa_oaep_md:sha256 -out session_key.enc

# 5. Send: session_key.enc + iv.hex + large_file.enc

# 6. Receiver decrypts session key with RSA Private Key
openssl pkeyutl -decrypt -in session_key.enc -inkey rsa_private.pem -pkeyopt rsa_padding_mode:oaep -pkeyopt rsa_oaep_md:sha256 -out session_key_decrypted.bin

# 7. Receiver decrypts file with session key
openssl enc -d -aes-256-gcm -K $(xxd -p -c 32 session_key_decrypted.bin) -iv $(cat iv.hex) -in large_file.enc -out large_file_decrypted.pdf
Part 12 — Summary and Glossary
Summary Comparison: Symmetric vs Asymmetric
Feature	Symmetric (AES-GCM, ChaCha20)	Asymmetric (RSA, ECC)
Keys	One shared secret key	Public / Private key pair
Speed	Extremely Fast (GB/s)	Slow (KB/s, ms per op)
Key Distribution	Hard (Secure channel needed)	Easy (Public key is public)
Scalability	O(N^2) keys for N parties	O(N) keys for N parties
Primary Use	Bulk Data Encryption	Key Exchange, Signatures, Identity
Key Size (128-bit sec)	128 bits	RSA-3072 / ECC-256
Quantum Resistance	Grover’s Algorithm (sqrt speedup)	Shor’s Algorithm (Broken)
Glossary of Key Terms
Plaintext: Readable, unencrypted data.
Ciphertext: Encrypted, unreadable data.
Symmetric Encryption: Encryption using a single shared key for both encryption and decryption.
Asymmetric Encryption: Encryption using a mathematically linked public/private key pair.
AES (Advanced Encryption Standard): The global standard symmetric block cipher (128-bit blocks).
GCM (Galois/Counter Mode): Authenticated Encryption mode providing Confidentiality + Integrity.
ChaCha20-Poly1305: Modern stream cipher AEAD, fast in software, used in TLS 1.3.
RSA: Asymmetric algorithm based on integer factorization. Legacy standard.
ECC (Elliptic Curve Cryptography): Asymmetric algorithm based on elliptic curve discrete log. Modern standard.
ECDH / X25519: Elliptic Curve Diffie-Hellman Key Agreement. Provides Perfect Forward Secrecy.
Digital Signature: Asymmetric operation providing Authentication, Integrity, Non-Repudiation.
Ed25519 / EdDSA: Modern deterministic elliptic curve signature scheme. Gold standard.
Hybrid Encryption: Encrypting data with a symmetric key, then encrypting that key asymmetrically.
Perfect Forward Secrecy (PFS): Compromise of long-term keys does not compromise past session keys.
AEAD (Authenticated Encryption with Associated Data): Encryption providing Confidentiality + Integrity + Authenticity simultaneously.
Nonce / IV: A number used once. Critical for security. Reuse is catastrophic in stream ciphers / AEAD.
Padding Oracle: Attack on unauthenticated decryption (CBC) that leaks plaintext via error messages.
Perfect Forward Secrecy (PFS): Compromise of long-term keys does not compromise past session keys.
Key Derivation Function (KDF): Derives strong keys from passwords or shared secrets (HKDF, Argon2).
