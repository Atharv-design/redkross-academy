Part 1 — Hook: The Collapse of Single-Factor Authentication and the Modern Credential Crisis

In September 2022, a 18-year-old threat actor affiliated with the Lapsus$ hacking group breached the internal network of ride-hailing giant Uber. The attacker did not write a complex zero-day exploit, nor did they crack a 256-bit encryption key. Instead, they purchased a valid set of corporate credentials belonging to an Uber contractor on a dark web marketplace for less than $10. These credentials had been harvested weeks earlier by an infostealer malware infection on the contractor’s personal device.

However, Uber’s internal systems were protected by Multi-Factor Authentication (MFA). When the attacker attempted to log in with the stolen username and password, the system issued a mobile push notification to the contractor’s smartphone, asking them to approve the login request. The contractor initially rejected the prompt.

The attacker did not give up. They launched an attack vector known as MFA Fatigue (or Push Prompt Bombing). The attacker script triggered over 100 consecutive login requests in the middle of the night, flooding the contractor’s phone with push notifications every few seconds. Concurrently, the attacker contacted the contractor on WhatsApp, impersonating Uber’s internal IT Security team, and messaged: “Your phone is malfunctioning due to a system glitch. To stop the notifications, please approve the pending request.”

Exhausted, confused, and wanting the noise to stop, the contractor tapped “Approve.” With that single tap, the attacker bypassed MFA, enrolled their own device, accessed Uber’s internal network, discovered hardcoded admin credentials in an internal network share, and gained full control over Uber’s AWS cloud infrastructure, Google Workspace, and Slack channels.

[ Stolen Password ] ──> [ Attacker Triggers 100+ Push Prompts ] ──> [ Victim Exhausted at 2 AM ] ──> [ Taps "Approve" ] ──> [ FULL NETWORK BREACH ]

Two years earlier, in July 2020, Twitter (now X) suffered a devastating security incident where attackers took over 130 high-profile accounts—including those of Barack Obama, Elon Musk, Bill Gates, and Apple—to execute a massive cryptocurrency scam. The entry vector? A combination of SIM Swapping and social engineering aimed at Twitter employees. The attackers called employees pretending to be helpdesk staff, tricked them into visiting a phishing site, and intercepted the SMS-based Multi-Factor Authentication codes sent to their phones by taking control of their cellular SIM profiles at the telecommunications carrier.

These real-world incidents reveal two brutal realities of modern cybersecurity:

Passwords alone are completely broken. Single-factor authentication is incapable of protecting modern systems against automated credential stuffing, infostealers, and phishing.
Not all MFA is created equal. Weak or legacy MFA mechanisms—such as SMS One-Time Passwords (OTPs) and basic Push Notifications—can be bypassed via SIM swapping, social engineering, Adversary-in-the-Middle (AiTM) phishing proxies, and prompt exhaustion.

To build resilient identity architectures, security engineers must understand the deep mechanics, mathematical models, and threat vectors of various Multi-Factor Authentication mechanisms, as well as the cryptographic architecture of modern Password Managers.

Part 4 — The Mathematical Necessity of MFA & Factor Independence

Single-factor authentication relies entirely on a single secret—typically a password (“something you know”). The probability of an account being compromised under single-factor authentication ($P(C_{SF})$) is equal to the probability that an attacker discovers, guesses, phishes, or extracts that single secret:

$$P(C_{SF}) = P(\text{Password Exposed})$$

Because password reuse is rampant (over 70% of users reuse passwords across multiple sites), and because infostealer malware (e.g., RedLine, Racoon) infects millions of endpoints annually, $P(\text{Password Exposed})$ approaches $1$ for a substantial percentage of any user population over time.

Multi-Factor Authentication & Factor Independence

Multi-Factor Authentication (MFA) requires the presentation of two or more independent authentication factors:

Factor 1 (Knowledge): Something you know (e.g., Password, PIN).
Factor 2 (Possession): Something you have (e.g., Hardware Key, Smartphone, TOTP App).
Factor 3 (Inherence): Something you are (e.g., Fingerprint, Facial Scan, Iris Pattern).

For an authentication architecture to qualify as true MFA, the factors must belong to different categories. Requiring a password and a PIN is NOT MFA—it is simply Two-Factor Knowledge (2FK) or double-password authentication, because an attacker who keylogs or phishes the user can capture both secrets simultaneously.

[ Password ] + [ PIN ]           = 2 Secrets, 1 Factor (Knowledge)   --> NOT MFA
[ Password ] + [ Hardware Key ]  = 2 Secrets, 2 Factors (Know + Have) --> TRUE MFA

Mathematically, if Factor 1 (Password) and Factor 2 (Hardware Token) are strictly independent, the probability of simultaneous compromise ($P(C_{MFA})$) is the product of their individual compromise probabilities:

$$P(C_{MFA}) = P(\text{Factor 1 Compromised}) \times P(\text{Factor 2 Compromised})$$

If the probability of a password leak is $P(F_1) = 0.5$ (50%) and the probability of a physical hardware token theft is $P(F_2) = 0.0001$ (0.01%), the total compromise probability drops dramatically:

$$P(C_{MFA}) = 0.5 \times 0.0001 = 0.00005 \quad (0.005\%)$$

According to data published by Microsoft and CISA, enabling any form of MFA blocks over 99.2% of automated account takeover attacks. However, as we will explore in the next section, sophisticated targeted attacks (such as AiTM phishing) can reduce factor independence if the chosen MFA channel transmits data across an insecure or non-origin-bound medium.

Extended Factors in Modern Architecture

Beyond the classic three factors, modern Zero-Trust identity architectures incorporate two additional contextual signals:

Factor 4 (Location / Geofencing): Somewhere you are (e.g., GPS coordinates, corporate IP subnet, trusted BGP network).
Factor 5 (Behavioral Biometrics): Something you do (e.g., Keystroke dynamics, mouse movement velocity, touchscreen swipe patterns).

1. Insecure Out-of-Band Channels: SMS and Voice Call OTPs

In an SMS-based MFA workflow, after entering a valid password, the web server generates a 6-digit numeric code, stores it in a temporary session cache with a short expiration time (e.g., 5 minutes), and sends it to the user’s registered mobile phone number via a Telecommunications SMS Gateway (e.g., Twilio).

Protocol & Architectural Vulnerabilities:
SIM Swapping (Carrier Social Engineering): Threat actors contact a mobile carrier’s customer support (or bribe a rogue retail store employee) and claim that “their” phone was lost. They convince the carrier to transfer the target’s phone number to a new SIM card in the attacker’s possession. Once transferred, all SMS MFA codes are delivered directly to the attacker’s device.
SS7 (Signaling System No. 7) Exploitation: SS7 is the legacy global telecommunications routing protocol used by cellular networks. SS7 lacks modern cryptographic authentication. Nation-states and cybercriminal groups with access to SS7 tracking services can reroute SMS traffic globally, intercepting OTPs silently without alerting the victim.
IMSI Catchers (“Stingrays”): Fake cellular towers operated locally by attackers that force nearby mobile devices to downgrade connections to unencrypted 2G channels, capturing SMS messages over the air.

NIST Special Publication 800-63B Status: NIST has officially classified SMS and Voice-based authentication as RESTRICTED channels, explicitly discouraging their use in federal and high-security enterprise systems.

2. Shared Secret Tokens: HOTP (RFC 4226) and TOTP (RFC 6238)

To eliminate dependency on cellular networks, the security community developed local, math-based One-Time Password algorithms that run inside authenticator apps (e.g., Google Authenticator, Bitwarden, Microsoft Authenticator).

A. HMAC-Based One-Time Password (HOTP - RFC 4226)

HOTP generates a single-use code based on an event counter $C$ and a shared secret key $K$ shared between the client app and the server.

$$\text{HOTP}(K, C) = \text{Truncate}(\text{HMAC-SHA-1}(K, C))$$
Client (Authenticator App)                    Server
  Shared Secret (K)                             Shared Secret (K)
  Counter (C = 1)                               Counter (C = 1)
        │                                              │
        ▼                                              ▼
  HMAC-SHA1(K, 1)                               HMAC-SHA1(K, 1)
        │                                              │
        ▼                                              ▼
  Truncate() ──> [ 482910 ] ──(Submits)──> Verify [ 482910 ] == [ 482910 ]
        │                                              │
  Increment C (C = 2)                            Increment C (C = 2)
Desynchronization Risk: If a user taps “Generate Code” on their phone 5 times without submitting it to the server, the client counter becomes $C=6$ while the server counter remains $C=1$. To fix this, servers implement a Look-Ahead Window ($s$), checking values for $C, C+1, C+2 \dots C+s$.
B. Time-Based One-Time Password (TOTP - RFC 6238)

TOTP is an extension of HOTP where the event counter $C$ is replaced by a time-based counter $T$, derived from the current Unix Epoch time.

$$T = \left\lfloor \frac{T_{\text{current}} - T_0}{X} \right\rfloor$$
$T_{\text{current}}$ = Current Unix timestamp in seconds (e.g., 1700000000).
$T_0$ = Initial Epoch time (default = 0).
$X$ = Time step interval in seconds (default = 30 seconds).
$$\text{TOTP}(K, T_{\text{current}}) = \text{HOTP}\left(K, \left\lfloor \frac{T_{\text{current}}}{30} \right\rfloor\right)$$
Step-by-Step Mathematical Derivation of a TOTP Code:

Let us trace how a phone app calculates a 6-digit TOTP code:

Shared Secret Decoding: The QR code scanned during enrollment contains a Secret Key $K$ encoded in Base32 (e.g., JBSWY3DPEHPK3PXP). The app decodes this string into raw binary bytes.
Time Counter Calculation: The app gets the current Unix time (e.g., $t = 1700000015$). It divides by $30$ and floors the result:

$$T = \lfloor 1700000015 / 30 \rfloor = 56666667$$
This integer $T$ is converted into an 8-byte big-endian binary array.
HMAC Computation: The app calculates the HMAC-SHA1 digest using key $K$ and message $T$:

$$\text{Hash} = \text{HMAC-SHA1}(K, T) \quad (\text{Produces a 20-byte / 160-bit digest})$$
Dynamic Truncation: To convert the 20-byte hash into a short code:
Take the last byte of the 20-byte hash: Offset = Hash[19] & 0x0F. (This yields a value between $0$ and $15$).
Extract 4 bytes from the hash starting at Offset: Result = Hash[Offset ... Offset+3].
Mask the most significant bit to avoid signed integer issues:

$$\text{BinaryCode} = (\text{Result}) \ \& \ \text{0x7FFFFFFF}$$
Modulo Reduction: Take the integer BinaryCode modulo $10^6$ ($1,000,000$) to get a 6-digit number:

$$\text{TOTP Code} = \text{BinaryCode} \pmod{10^6}$$
20-Byte HMAC Digest: [b0, b1, b2 ... b18, b19]
                                           │
                                    Offset = b19 & 0x0F (e.g., 10)
                                           │
Extract 4 Bytes:               [b10, b11, b12, b13]
                                           │
Mask MSB (0x7FFFFFFF) ──> Convert to Integer ──> Modulo 10^6 ──> "482910"
Vulnerability of TOTP:

While TOTP eliminates SIM swapping and telecom reliance, TOTP is still vulnerable to Adversary-in-the-Middle (AiTM) Phishing. If an attacker uses a reverse proxy tool like Evilginx2, the victim enters their 6-digit TOTP code into a fake login site. The proxy forwards the code to the real server in real-time within the 30-second window, authenticates successfully, and steals the resulting session cookie.

3. Out-of-Band Push Notifications & Prompt Bombing Countermeasures

Push notifications send an encrypted payload over Apple APNs or Google FCM to an authenticator app installed on the user’s phone, displaying a prompt: “Are you trying to sign in to Acme Corp?” with two buttons: [Approve] or [Deny].

Threat Vector: MFA Fatigue / Push Prompt Bombing

As illustrated in the Uber breach, attackers with valid passwords script automated systems to trigger dozens or hundreds of push notifications during off-hours. Users suffer cognitive fatigue or panic, eventually tapping Approve to silence the prompts.

Mitigation: Number Matching (Challenge-Response Push)

To render Push Notifications phishing-resistant against prompt bombing, modern identity platforms enforce Number Matching:

The web login screen displays a random 2-digit or 3-digit number (e.g., 84).
The user’s phone receives a push notification, but instead of [Approve]/[Deny] buttons, it presents an empty text field.
The user must look at the web screen and manually type 84 into the mobile app.
Web Browser Login Screen                   User Mobile Phone
┌──────────────────────────────┐           ┌──────────────────────────────┐
│ Enter the number shown below │           │ Authenticator App            │
│ into your Authenticator App: │           │                              │
│                              │           │ Enter the matching number:   │
│           [ 84 ]             │ ────────> │ [  84  ]                     │
│                              │           │                              │
└──────────────────────────────┘           │        [ Submit ]            │
                                           └──────────────────────────────┘

An attacker sitting in another country executing prompt bombing cannot see the number displayed on the victim’s local web screen, making blind approval impossible.

4. Phishing-Resistant Standard: FIDO2 / WebAuthn & Hardware Security Keys

To completely eradicate credential phishing, AiTM proxies, and SIM swapping, the FIDO Alliance and W3C created FIDO2 (comprising the WebAuthn browser API and the CTAP2 Client-to-Authenticator Protocol).

FIDO2 replaces shared secrets with Asymmetric Public-Key Cryptography bound directly to a specific domain name (Origin Binding).

Cryptographic Protocol Flow of FIDO2/WebAuthn:
User / Browser (Client)            Relying Party (Server)         Hardware Key (YubiKey)
        │                                    │                               │
        │─── 1. Initiate Login ─────────────>│                               │
        │                                    │                               │
        │<── 2. Challenge + RP ID ("a.com")──│                               │
        │                                    │                               │
        │─── 3. CTAP2 GetAssertion(Challenge, RP ID) ───────────────────────>│
        │                                                                    │ Verify User Touch
        │                                                                    │ Sign(Challenge + RP ID)
        │<── 4. Signed Assertion Signature ──────────────────────────────────│
        │                                    │                               │
        │─── 5. Submit Signed Assertion ────>│                               │
        │                                    │ Verify Signature with         │
        │<── 6. Auth Success + Session ──────│ Stored Public Key             │
Enrollment Phase:
The user connects a FIDO2 hardware key (e.g., YubiKey) or enables a platform authenticator (e.g., Apple TouchID/FaceID, Windows Hello).
The hardware key generates an asymmetric key pair (Public Key $PK$, Private Key $SK$) unique to that specific domain (Relying Party ID, e.g., login.company.com).
$SK$ is locked inside the tamper-proof hardware enclave and never leaves the device. $PK$ is sent to the server and stored in the user’s database record.
Authentication Phase:
The user attempts to log in to login.company.com.
The server issues a cryptographically random Challenge string and sends its Relying Party ID (RP ID: "login.company.com").
The browser passes this data to the hardware key via CTAP2.
The user touches the physical key (proving user presence/inherence).
The hardware key constructs a payload containing the Challenge + Relying Party ID + Authenticator Data, signs it using its private key $SK$, and returns the signature.
The server verifies the signature using the stored public key $PK$.
Why FIDO2/WebAuthn Completely Eradicates AiTM Phishing (Origin Binding):

Consider what happens when an attacker tricks a user into visiting a fake phishing site using Evilginx2 at login.company.com.phishing.attacker.com:

User on Fake Site: "login.company.com.phishing.attacker.com"
                             │
                             ▼
Browser extracts real address bar domain: "login.company.com.phishing.attacker.com"
                             │
                             ▼
Hardware Key receives RP ID: "login.company.com.phishing.attacker.com"
                             │
                             ▼
Hardware Key searches internal storage for keypair matching "login.company.com.phishing.attacker.com"
                             │
                             ▼
RESULT: NO KEY MATCH FOUND! (Or signs assertion for attacker's domain)
                             │
                             ▼
Attacker forwards payload to real server ("login.company.com")
Real Server sees RP ID mismatch: REJECTED!

Because the web browser automatically injects the exact, real domain string from the address bar into the WebAuthn API call, the hardware key signs the assertion for the phishing domain, not the real domain. When the attacker’s proxy relays this signature to the real server, the real server detects the domain mismatch and immediately drops the connection. FIDO2 is mathematically un-phishable via AiTM proxies.

Part 7 — Password Managers & Credential Hygiene Architecture

Even with MFA deployed, strong, unique passwords remain a critical baseline layer of defense in depth. However, human cognitive limitations create a fundamental conflict between security policies and human memory.

The Cognitive Entropy Paradox & Miller’s Law

Psychologist George Miller established Miller’s Law, which states that the human short-term memory can retain only $7 \pm 2$ chunks of information at a time.

To achieve 128 bits of entropy (the standard for mathematical unpredictability), a random alphanumeric password must be at least 22 characters long. A human managing 100 distinct web accounts would need to memorize 100 different 22-character random strings—requiring $100 \times 22 = 2,200$ chunks of random data.

Because this is cognitively impossible, humans without password managers resort to dangerous coping mechanisms:

Password Reuse: Using P@ssword2023! across 50 different websites. (A single breach on an insecure forum exposes all 50 accounts).
Predictable Transformations: Changing P@ssword2023! to P@ssword2024! for new registrations.
Short Passwords: Picking 8-character words that can be brute-forced on GPUs in seconds.

Password Managers resolve this paradox by shifting the memory burden from human brains to an encrypted cryptographic database (“Vault”). The user memorizes one single, high-entropy Master Password, and the software manages thousands of unique, 32-character, cryptographically random passwords automatically.

Zero-Knowledge Architecture of a Modern Password Vault

A secure password manager (e.g., Bitwarden, 1Password, KeePassXC) operates on a Zero-Knowledge Architecture. This means the service provider’s servers store only encrypted blobs of data and have zero mathematical ability to read, decrypt, or access the user’s vault contents.

The Cryptographic Encryption Pipeline:
[ Master Password (P) ] ──+── [ User Salt ]
                          │
                          ▼
            [ Key Derivation Function ]
            (Argon2id or PBKDF2 600k rounds)
                          │
                          ▼
              [ Derived Key (K_derived) ]
                          │
       ┌──────────────────┴──────────────────┐
       ▼                                     ▼
 [ Symmetric Key (K_sym) ]        [ Authentication Hash ]
       │                                     │
       ▼                                     ▼
 [ Encrypt Vault Blob ]           [ Sent to Server to Authenticate ]
 (AES-256-GCM / ChaCha20)          (Server NEVER sees K_sym!)
       │
       ▼
 [ Encrypted Ciphertext Blob ] ──> Sent to Cloud Server DB
Step-by-Step Cryptographic Breakdown:
Master Password Entry ($P$): The user enters their Master Password on their local client device. $P$ is NEVER transmitted across the network or written to disk.
Key Derivation: The client device runs a CPU/memory-hard Key Derivation Function (KDF) using the Master Password $P$ and a unique, account-specific Salt ($S$):

$$K_{\text{derived}} = \text{Argon2id}(P, S, m=64\text{MB}, t=3, p=4)$$
Key Splitting / Derivation of Sub-Keys: The resulting $K_{\text{derived}}$ bytes are split or run through HKDF (HMAC-based Extract-and-Expand Key Derivation Function) to produce two distinct keys:
Symmetric Vault Encryption Key ($K_{\text{sym}}$): Used exclusively for encrypting/decrypting local vault data.
Server Authentication Hash ($H_{\text{auth}}$): A separate hash sent over TLS to the server to verify the user’s identity during login.
$$\text{Server Authentication Hash} = \text{PBKDF2}(K_{\text{derived}}, P, \text{iterations}=1)$$
Vault Encryption: The local database containing all stored site credentials, notes, and TOTP seeds is encrypted using AES-256-GCM or ChaCha20-Poly1305 with $K_{\text{sym}}$.
Syncing: The application uploads only the encrypted ciphertext blob and $H_{\text{auth}}$ to the cloud server.
Why Zero-Knowledge Holds Under Breach:

In November 2022, cloud password manager provider LastPass suffered a major breach where attackers exfiltrated cloud backup volumes containing millions of customer encrypted vault blobs. Because LastPass utilized Zero-Knowledge encryption, the attackers obtained only the encrypted ciphertexts.

Customers who had used strong Master Passwords (generating high-entropy $K_{\text{derived}}$ via PBKDF2) remained safe because cracking the AES-256-GCM ciphertexts offline would require millions of dollars in GPU electricity per vault. Conversely, customers with short, weak Master Passwords had their vaults cracked offline using Hashcat wordlists.

Part 8 — Attacker vs Defender Viewpoint
The Attacker’s Exploitation Playbook
ATTACKER TOOLKIT
 ├─ Evilginx2 Proxy ──────────> Intercepts TOTP / Push MFA Cookies in Real-time
 ├─ Infostealer Malware ──────> Extracts Chrome/Edge Passwords via DPAPI APIs
 ├─ SIM Swap Automation ──────> Hijacks SMS OTP Channels via Telecommunications APIs
 └─ MFA Prompt Bombing ───────> Scripts 100+ Push Requests to force victim approval
Adversary-in-the-Middle (AiTM) Phishing (e.g., Evilginx2):
The attacker sets up a reverse proxy server at login.company.com.phishing.net.
When the victim visits, Evilginx2 fetches the real login page from login.company.com and displays it to the victim.
The victim types their username, password, and 6-digit TOTP code.
Evilginx2 passes these credentials to the real site, captures the resulting session_cookie returned by the server, and saves it.
The attacker imports the stolen session_cookie into their own browser, completely bypassing both the password and the TOTP MFA.
Credential Stuffing via Automated Botnets:
Attackers take 500 million leaked credentials from historic breaches (e.g., Collection #1 leak) and run multi-threaded tools (e.g., OpenBullet) to test those username/password pairs across thousands of bank and retail portals simultaneously.
Infostealers (e.g., RedLine, Vidar, Lumma):
Trojanized software or malicious Google Ads trick users into running an executable. The malware scans local disk paths for browser SQLite databases, calls Windows DPAPI to decrypt stored credentials, scrapes active browser session cookies, and zips the data into a “log” sent to a Telegram bot or C2 server.
The Defender’s Hardening Playbook
Enforce FIDO2 / WebAuthn for High-Risk Users: Eliminate TOTP and SMS for administrators, finance staff, and executives, replacing them with hardware security keys or Passkeys.
Deploy Number Matching for Push MFA: Configure identity providers (Microsoft Entra ID, Okta) to require 2-digit number matching for all push prompts, defeating MFA fatigue.
Migrate to Zero-Knowledge Enterprise Password Managers: Mandate the use of Bitwarden or 1Password across the organization. Block browser-builtin password saving via Group Policy / MDM.
Implement Conditional Access Policies: Require that logins originate from compliant, MDM-managed corporate devices and trusted IP ranges. If an attacker steals a password and TOTP code, the login fails because their device is not MDM-enrolled.
Set High Entropy Parameters: Configure Password Manager accounts to use Argon2id (64MB RAM, 3 iterations) or PBKDF2 with at least 600,000 iterations.
Part 9 — Common Implementation Anti-Patterns & Pitfalls
Anti-Pattern 1: Insecure SMS Fallback on FIDO2/TOTP Accounts
The Vulnerability: An organization enforces FIDO2 WebAuthn for login, but includes an “I lost my key” link that sends a 6-digit OTP via SMS to the user’s mobile phone.
The Impact: The security level of the account instantly collapses to the security level of the weakest enabled factor (SMS). An attacker SIM swaps the target and uses the “Lost Key” SMS workflow to bypass FIDO2 entirely.
Anti-Pattern 2: Collocating TOTP Secrets inside the Password Manager Vault
The Practice: Many cloud password managers offer an internal TOTP generator feature, storing the Base32 TOTP secret $K$ inside the same item entry as the site password.
The Vulnerability: Storing Factor 1 (Password) and Factor 2 (TOTP Secret) in the exact same vault destroys factor independence. If an attacker compromises the password manager vault (e.g., via a weak master password or RAM stealer), they obtain both factors simultaneously.
Remediation: Keep TOTP secrets in an independent authenticator app or hardware token separate from the password manager.
Anti-Pattern 3: Insecure Master Password Recovery Flows
The Practice: Allowing users to reset their Password Manager Master Password via an email link or security questions (“What was your first pet’s name?”).
The Impact: Violates Zero-Knowledge architecture. If the company can reset the master password via email, the company possesses the technical capability to decrypt the vault, making it vulnerable to subpoenas, insider threats, and email account compromise.
Anti-Pattern 4: Unlimited Push MFA Retries
The Practice: Allowing an application to trigger unlimited push MFA notifications per minute without rate-limiting or lockout mechanisms.
Remediation: Enforce exponential backoff and lock account MFA triggers after 5 unapproved push prompts within 10 minutes.

Lab Exercise 1: Pure Python RFC 6238 TOTP Engine

Create a script named custom_totp.py to calculate TOTP codes from scratch without third-party libraries:

python
import base64
import hashlib
import hmac
import struct
import time

def base32_decode(secret_base32):
    """Decodes a Base32 secret string into raw binary bytes."""
    # Add padding if necessary
    missing_padding = len(secret_base32) % 8
    if missing_padding != 0:
        secret_base32 += '=' * (8 - missing_padding)
    return base64.b32decode(secret_base32, casefold=True)

def generate_hotp(secret_bytes, counter):
    """
    Generates an HOTP code using RFC 4226 HMAC-SHA1.
    """
    # 1. Convert counter integer into 8-byte big-endian binary array
    counter_bytes = struct.pack(">Q", counter)
    
    # 2. Calculate HMAC-SHA1
    hmac_digest = hmac.new(secret_bytes, counter_bytes, hashlib.sha1).digest()
    
    # 3. Dynamic Truncation: Get offset from last nibble (lowest 4 bits)
    offset = hmac_digest[-1] & 0x0F
    
    # 4. Extract 4 bytes from hash starting at offset
    four_bytes = hmac_digest[offset:offset + 4]
    
    # 5. Convert to 32-bit unsigned integer and mask Most Significant Bit
    code_int = struct.unpack(">I", four_bytes)[0] & 0x7FFFFFFF
    
    # 6. Take modulo 10^6 to get 6-digit OTP
    otp = code_int % 1000000
    return f"{otp:06d}"

def generate_totp(secret_base32, time_step=30):
    """
    Generates a TOTP code using RFC 6238.
    """
    secret_bytes = base32_decode(secret_base32)
    current_unix_time = int(time.time())
    
    # Calculate Time Counter T = floor(Current_Time / Time_Step)
    time_counter = current_unix_time // time_step
    
    # Calculate remaining seconds in current 30s window
    remaining_seconds = time_step - (current_unix_time % time_step)
    
    otp_code = generate_hotp(secret_bytes, time_counter)
    return otp_code, remaining_seconds

Create a script named entropy_auditor.py to evaluate the Shannon Entropy of generated passwords:

python
import math
import string

def calculate_shannon_entropy(password):
    """
    Calculates Shannon Entropy in bits: H(X) = - SUM( P(x) * log2(P(x)) )
    """
    if not password:
        return 0.0
    
    length = len(password)
    char_counts = {}
    
    for char in password:
        char_counts[char] = char_counts.get(char, 0) + 1
        
    entropy = 0.0
    for count in char_counts.values():
        probability = count / length
        entropy -= probability * math.log2(probability)
        
    # Total entropy in bits = character entropy * length
    total_entropy_bits = entropy * length
    return total_entropy_bits

def analyze_password(password):
    total_bits = calculate_shannon_entropy(password)
    print(f"\nPassword : '{password}'")
    print(f"Length   : {len(password)} chars")
    print(f"Entropy  : {total_bits:.2f} bits")
    
    if total_bits < 40:
        print("Verdict  : CRITICAL WEAKNESS (Crackable in seconds)")
    elif total_bits < 70:
        print("Verdict  : MODERATE (Vulnerable to GPU dictionary rules)")
    else:
        print("Verdict  : STRONG (Cryptographically resilient)")

Glossary of Key Terms
Multi-Factor Authentication (MFA): An authentication architecture requiring two or more independent factors from different categories (Know, Have, Are).
HOTP (RFC 4226): HMAC-Based One-Time Password algorithm utilizing a shared secret key and an incrementing event counter.
TOTP (RFC 6238): Time-Based One-Time Password algorithm utilizing a shared secret key and current Unix Epoch time steps (typically 30 seconds).
Adversary-in-the-Middle (AiTM) Phishing: A proxy attack (e.g., Evilginx2) that intercepts credentials and MFA codes in real-time, stealing active session cookies.
MFA Fatigue (Push Prompt Bombing): An attack technique where automated scripts flood a victim with push notifications to force accidental approval.
Number Matching: A push MFA countermeasure requiring the user to type a number displayed on the web login screen into their mobile authenticator app.
FIDO2 / WebAuthn: An open authentication standard utilizing asymmetric public-key cryptography bound directly to the browser domain name (Origin Binding).
Origin Binding: The cryptographic mechanism in FIDO2 that binds WebAuthn credentials to a specific domain name, rendering phishing domains mathematically ineffective.
Zero-Knowledge Architecture: A vault security model where data is encrypted/decrypted locally on the client device, ensuring the cloud server never possesses the decryption keys.
SIM Swapping: A social engineering attack targeting mobile carriers to transfer a target’s phone number to an attacker-controlled SIM card.
