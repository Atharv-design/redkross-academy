Lesson 2: AAA and Non-Repudiation
Part 1 — Hook

You walk into a bank. The guard at the door asks for your ID. You show it. He checks the photo against your face, and he lets you in. That is authentication: proving you are who you say you are.

Inside, you go to the teller. She checks your ID again, then checks your account balance, and then she lets you withdraw money. That is authorization: deciding what you are allowed to do.

After the transaction, the bank prints a receipt and stores a record in its computer. If you later say “I never withdrew that money,” the bank can produce the receipt and the record and prove that you did. That is accounting — and that is non-repudiation: proving that something happened, so nobody can honestly deny it.

Three letters describe the first three steps: AAA. Authentication, Authorization, and Accounting. They are the plumbing of every system that knows who did what. If the plumbing leaks, everything above it floods.

This lesson is about that plumbing. It is less glamorous than hacking and less exciting than zero trust, but it is the foundation. Every security failure you will ever study — every breach, every fraud, every unauthorised access — traces back to a failure in one of these three letters.

Part 2 — Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

Define AAA — Authentication, Authorization, and Accounting — in simple terms.
Explain each of the three A’s, with at least two real examples of each.
Distinguish between identification, authentication, and authorization.
Explain non-repudiation and why it is different from the three A’s.
Describe how digital signatures provide non-repudiation.
Identify AAA failures in real-world scenarios.

Part 4 — Concept from Scratch

Here is the central sentence of this lesson.

AAA is the system that answers three questions in order: Who are you? What can you do? And what did you do? If you cannot answer all three, you do not have a security system. You have a door with no lock.

Let me say that again, because it is the sentence you will carry into every future lesson.

AAA is the system that answers three questions in order: Who are you? What can you do? And what did you do?

Notice the word “in order.” These three questions are not a menu. They are a sequence. You cannot decide what someone is allowed to do until you know who they are. You cannot record what they did until you know who they are and what they were allowed to do. Break the sequence and the whole system collapses.

Part 5 — How It Works: The Three A’s, One by One
The First A: Authentication — Who Are You?

Authentication is the process of proving your identity. It is the first gate. Nothing happens until the system knows — or believes — who you are.

How authentication works. You claim an identity. You provide evidence. The system checks the evidence. If the evidence is convincing, you are authenticated. If not, you are stopped.

The three types of evidence. This is the most important framework in this lesson. Every authentication system on earth uses some combination of these three:

Something you know. A password, a PIN, a security question answer, a passphrase. This is the weakest type, because it can be stolen, guessed, or observed. But it is the most common, because it is the cheapest.
Something you have. A phone, a security token, a smart card, a key fob, an authenticator app. This is stronger than something you know, because the attacker needs to physically take it from you. But it can be lost or stolen.
Something you are. A fingerprint, a face, a retina, a voice, a gait. This is the strongest type, because it is attached to your body. But it is the most expensive, and it has privacy concerns — your biometric data, once leaked, cannot be changed.

Multi-factor authentication (MFA) is the combination of two or more of these. A password plus a code sent to your phone is MFA: something you know plus something you have. MFA is the single most effective security control available, because it stops almost all phishing and password theft. If you take nothing else from this lesson, take this: use MFA everywhere you can.

Real examples of authentication:

You type your username and password into your email. The system checks the password against its records. You are authenticated.
You insert your bank card into an ATM and enter your PIN. The card is something you have. The PIN is something you know. You are authenticated.
You place your finger on your phone’s fingerprint sensor. The sensor matches the print against the stored template. You are authenticated.
You receive a code in an authenticator app and type it into a login page. The code proves you have the phone that was registered to the account. You are authenticated.

What happens when authentication fails: Someone gets in who should not. This is the most common cause of security breaches. The attacker does not break the encryption. They steal the password, guess it, or trick the user into giving it away. Every phishing attack is an authentication failure. The attacker authenticated as someone else.

The Second A: Authorization — What Can You Do?

Authorization is the process of deciding what you are allowed to do. It comes after authentication, and it is the part that most users never see.

How authorization works. The system looks at your identity. It looks at a set of rules. It decides whether your request is allowed. If yes, you get access. If no, you get a “permission denied” message.

The models of authorization. There are four standard models, and you need to know them because they appear in every professional context.

DAC — Discretionary Access Control. The owner of a resource decides who can access it. A file on your computer is DAC: you decide who can read it. DAC is flexible, but it is weak, because the owner might make a bad decision. Most personal computers use DAC.

MAC — Mandatory Access Control. The system decides, based on rules set by a central authority, not by the owner. The owner cannot override the rules. MAC is used in military and government systems, where a soldier with a “secret” clearance cannot read a “top secret” file, no matter what the file owner wants. MAC is strong, but it is rigid.

RBAC — Role-Based Access Control. Access is granted based on your role in the organisation. A marketing person can access marketing data. A finance person can access finance data. RBAC is the most common model in business, because it is both manageable and sensible. You studied this in Module 1 Lesson 7.

ABAC — Attribute-Based Access Control. Access is granted based on attributes: who you are, what you are doing, where you are, what time it is, and what the data contains. ABAC is the most flexible model, and it is used for complex environments. A doctor can access a patient’s record only if the doctor is treating that patient, in that hospital, during that shift. That is ABAC.

Real examples of authorization:

You log into your university portal. You can see your grades. You cannot see other students’ grades. That is authorization.
A file on a shared drive has a permission list. The marketing team can edit it. The sales team can only read it. Everyone else gets nothing. That is authorization.
A bank customer can check their balance. The same customer cannot transfer money from someone else’s account. That is authorization.
A junior developer can read the source code repository. The same developer cannot deploy to the production server. That is authorization.

What happens when authorization fails: Someone does something they should not. This is the second most common cause of breaches, after authentication failure. The classic example is IDOR — Insecure Direct Object Reference — where a user changes a number in a URL and accesses another user’s data. The attacker was authorized to access the page. They were not authorized to access that specific record.

The Third A: Accounting — What Did You Do?

Accounting is the process of recording what happened. It is the least visible of the three A’s, and it is the most important, because without it you cannot investigate anything.

How accounting works. The system creates a record of every significant event: who logged in, what they accessed, what they changed, when they did it, and from where. These records are called logs, and they are the most valuable data in security.

What accounting records. This is not a complete list, but it gives you the idea:

Authentication events: successful logins, failed logins, password changes.
Authorization events: access denied, privilege escalation.
Data events: files created, modified, deleted, copied.
Administrative events: rules changed, users created, permissions modified.
Network events: connections made, blocked, scanned.

Why accounting matters. Without logs, you are blind. You cannot tell whether an attack happened. You cannot tell who did what. You cannot investigate after the fact. You cannot comply with regulations that require audit trails. The SolarWinds attack from Module 1 was discovered by a security company that was actively monitoring its logs. If nobody had been logging, the attack would have gone undetected for years.

Real examples of accounting:

Your email service records that you logged in from a new city. It sends you a notification: “New login detected.” That notification exists because of accounting.
A bank detects a fraudulent credit card transaction. It knows to call you because its accounting system noticed a purchase in a country you have never visited. That is accounting.
A company investigates a data leak. It pulls the access logs and finds that a specific employee downloaded the customer database at 2:00 AM the night before they resigned. That is accounting.
A hospital is audited. It produces logs showing that every access to patient records was justified. That is accounting.

What happens when accounting fails: Nobody knows what happened. The attack goes undetected. The attacker covers their tracks by deleting logs. The organisation discovers the breach months later from an external source. The breach response is slow and incomplete, because there is no record to work from.

Part 6 — Non-Repudiation: The Fourth Goal

Now let us talk about the idea that sits just outside the three A’s: non-repudiation.

What non-repudiation means. Non-repudiation is the ability to prove that someone did something, so that they cannot later honestly deny it. The word literally means “not denying.”

Why it is different from AAA. Authentication proves who you are at the moment of login. Authorization decides what you can do. Accounting records what happened. Non-repudiation takes that record and makes it legally and practically undeniable. It is the difference between “we think you did it” and “we can prove you did it, in a way that holds up in court.”

How non-repudiation works. There are three standard methods:

Digital signatures. A digital signature is a mathematical proof that a document came from a specific person and has not been changed. It works using public-key cryptography: the signer has a private key that nobody else has. They sign the document with that key. Everyone else has the corresponding public key, and they can verify the signature. If the signature verifies, the document definitely came from the signer, and it has not been modified. The signer cannot later deny signing it, because only they had the private key.
Timestamps. A timestamp proves that an action happened at a specific time. If you submit a bid at 2:03 PM, and the timestamp says 2:03 PM, you cannot later claim you submitted it at 2:30 PM. Timestamps are essential in financial transactions, legal evidence, and any situation where the order of events matters.
Audit trails. A complete, tamper-evident record of who did what, when, and from where. If the audit trail is properly maintained — if it cannot be altered without detection — it provides non-repudiation. This is why logs must be protected from modification. An attacker who can edit the logs destroys the non-repudiation value of everything the logs recorded.

Real examples of non-repudiation:

You sign a contract electronically. The platform creates a digital signature with a timestamp. Months later, you cannot claim you never signed it.
A bank processes a money transfer. The sender cannot later claim the transfer never happened, because the bank has a signed receipt and an audit trail.
A software developer commits code to a repository. The commit is signed with their GPG key. Everyone can see that the developer wrote that code. The developer cannot deny it.
A patient signs a consent form for surgery. The hospital stores the signed form. If the patient later claims they never consented, the hospital produces the form.

Why non-repudiation matters. It is the difference between an internal system and a trustworthy system. An internal system can record what happened and use it for investigation. A trustworthy system can prove what happened to a third party — a court, a regulator, a customer. This distinction is why financial systems, legal systems, and healthcare systems invest so heavily in audit trails and digital signatures.

Part 8 — Attack and Defence View

Let us now look at AAA from the attacker’s perspective, because that is how you learn to defend.

The attacker attacks all four. Every attack in the kill chain from Module 1 is an attack on one or more of these.

Attack authentication. Steal a password. Phish a user. Brute-force a PIN. Crack a hash. The attacker becomes someone else.
Attack authorization. Find an IDOR. Exploit a broken access control. Escalate privileges. The attacker does something they should not.
Attack accounting. Delete the logs. Disable monitoring. Cover the tracks. The attacker disappears without a trace.
Attack non-repudiation. Forge a signature. Tamper with a timestamp. The attacker makes the evidence useless.

The defender’s job is to protect all four, in order. Strong authentication stops the attacker at the first gate. Strong authorization stops them once they are inside. Strong accounting means that if they get past the first two, you will know. Strong non-repudiation means that if they get past all three, you can still prove what they did.

The most common AAA failure pattern is simple: weak authentication, weak authorization, and no accounting. The organisation has a password system that anyone can guess, a permission system that gives everyone too much access, and no logs. The attacker walks in through the first gate, walks wherever they want, and leaves no trace. This describes a huge number of real breaches, including many of the ones we studied in Module 1.

The fix is not expensive. It is discipline. Require MFA. Apply least privilege. Enable logging and protect the logs. These three things would prevent the majority of breaches we studied, if they had been in place.

Part 9 — Common Mistakes Beginners Make

Mistake 1: Confusing identification with authentication. Identification is claiming who you are — typing a username. Authentication is proving it — providing a password. You can identify yourself as anyone. Authentication is what makes the claim credible. If you confuse the two, you will think a login page is secure when it is just asking for a name.

Mistake 2: Thinking authentication is enough. It is not. A properly authenticated user can still do enormous damage if they are authorized to access everything. The insider threat from Module 1 Lesson 6 is an authenticated user doing unauthorised things. Authentication is the first gate. It is not the whole building.

Mistake 3: Ignoring accounting because it is boring. This is the most common mistake, and it is the most expensive. Logs are not exciting. Nobody gets a promotion for “better logging.” But when the breach hits, the team that has logs investigates in hours. The team without logs investigates in weeks, and may never find the answer. Accounting is the least glamorous part of AAA and the most important.

Mistake 4: Treating non-repudiation as a technical problem. It is partly technical, but it is also legal. A digital signature is only as good as the law that recognises it. An audit trail is only useful if it is handled in a way that preserves its evidentiary value. If you work in finance, law, or healthcare, non-repudiation is a legal requirement, not a technical preference.

Mistake 5: Confusing AAA with CIA. They are related but different. AAA is about identity and access. CIA is about data properties. Authentication protects confidentiality (only the right people see it). Authorization protects all three. Accounting supports integrity and non-repudiation. Availability is mostly outside AAA. They are different frameworks that overlap, and you need to know both.

Part 10 — Guided Mini-Lab: Audit Your Own Access

Let us now apply AAA to your own life. This takes 15 minutes.

Step 1: List your accounts.
Write down your three most important online accounts: your email, your bank, and your cloud storage or social media.

Step 2: Check authentication.
For each account, ask:

Do I have a strong, unique password? (Not reused anywhere else.)
Is multi-factor authentication enabled? If not, enable it now.
Do I have a password manager storing this password, or do I remember it?

Step 3: Check authorization.
For each account, ask:

What can I do in this account? Can I change the password? Can I add other users? Can I delete everything?
Is there anyone else who has access to this account? A partner? A family member? An assistant? If yes, do I trust them with everything they can do?
Is there any setting that gives me more access than I need? An admin role I never use?

Step 4: Check accounting.
For each account, ask:

Does the service record logins and notify me of new ones? If yes, have I reviewed the login history recently?
If my account were compromised, would I know? Is there a list of active sessions or devices?

Step 5: Check non-repudiation.
For each account, ask:

If someone makes a transaction or sends a message from my account, can the service prove it was me? Is there a receipt, a timestamp, or a signed record?

Step 6: Write it down.
Open your lab notebook and write:

Part 11 — Why This Matters for the Rest of Module 2

Let me say something plainly.

AAA is the machinery that every other principle in Module 2 plugs into. Least privilege is about authorization — giving people exactly what they need. Zero trust is about authentication — verifying every request instead of trusting by default. Separation of duties is about authorization — ensuring that no single person controls a complete transaction. Secure defaults is about all three — starting with the safest settings instead of requiring the user to be careful.

If you understand AAA, the rest of Module 2 is a set of variations on a theme. Each principle is an answer to the same question: how do we make sure the right people can do the right things, and prove that they did?

Part 12 — Summary and Glossary

Here is what we covered.

AAA is the identity and access framework. Authentication proves who you are, using something you know, something you have, or something you are. Authorization decides what you can do, using DAC, MAC, RBAC, or ABAC. Accounting records what happened, in logs and audit trails.

Non-repudiation is the fourth goal: the ability to prove that someone did something, so they cannot deny it. It is provided by digital signatures, timestamps, and protected audit trails. It is the difference between an internal record and evidence that holds up in court.

The attacker attacks all four. The defender protects them in order. The most common failure pattern is weak authentication, weak authorization, and no accounting. The fix is discipline: MFA, least privilege, and protected logs.

Glossary of 8 terms:

Authentication — proving who you are.
Authorization — deciding what you are allowed to do.
Accounting — recording what you did.
Non-repudiation — proving that someone did something, so they cannot deny it.
MFA — multi-factor authentication; using two or more types of evidence.
RBAC — Role-Based Access Control; permissions based on your role.
Digital signature — a mathematical proof that a document came from a specific person.
Audit trail — a complete, tamper-evident record of events.
