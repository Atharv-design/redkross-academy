Lesson 5: Zero Trust
Part 1 — Hook

Imagine a company where nobody is trusted by default. Not the CEO. Not the network engineer. Not the cleaning staff. Not the person who has worked there for twenty years.

Every time anyone wants to do anything — open a file, send an email, access the database, reboot a server — they have to prove, again, that they are who they say they are, and that what they want to do is something they are allowed to do. Every single time. From every single device, in every single location.

That sounds exhausting, and for a long time it was considered impossible. The old model was “trust but verify”: once you are inside the network, you are trusted. The network perimeter was the wall, and everyone inside the wall was a friend.

Then the breaches came. Target. SolarWinds. The pandemic rush to remote work. And the old model collapsed, because it turned out that the wall was full of holes, and the friends inside the wall were sometimes the attackers.

Zero trust is the replacement. It says: trust nothing and nobody by default. Verify every request, every time, from every source. Assume the attacker may already be inside. Because they probably are.

Part 3 — Prerequisites

You have finished Module 1 and Module 2 Lessons 1 to 4. You know the CIA triad, AAA, least privilege, and defence in depth. Zero trust is what you get when you apply least privilege to every single request, not just to the people.

Part 4 — Concept from Scratch

Here is the central sentence of this lesson.

Zero trust is the principle of never trusting and always verifying: no user, no device, no application, and no network request is trusted by default, regardless of where it comes from or who sent it.

Let me say that again, because it is the most important sentence in this module and it contradicts a century of how networks were built.

Zero trust is the principle of never trusting and always verifying: no user, no device, no application, and no network request is trusted by default, regardless of where it comes from or who sent it.

The old model was built on geography. If you were inside the office, you were trusted. If you were outside, you had to prove yourself at the door. That model died between 2013 and 2020, and nobody noticed until it was too late. The reasons are simple:

The perimeter disappeared. Employees started working from home, from coffee shops, from airports. The office was no longer a physical place. The “inside” of the network became a concept, not a location.
The attacker got inside. The Target breach and the SolarWinds attack both succeeded because the attacker was already inside the perimeter. Once inside, they were trusted, because the network did not distinguish between an employee and an attacker.
Credentials were stolen. Phishing became industrial. A stolen password was a key to the kingdom, and the network could not tell the difference between the real employee and the person holding the stolen password.

Zero trust is the answer to all three. It removes geography from the equation entirely. It does not matter where you are. It only matters who you are, what device you are using, and what you are trying to do.

Part 5 — How It Works: The Core Principles of Zero Trust

Zero trust is not one thing. It is a set of principles that together remove trust from the equation. Let us go through them one by one.

Principle 1: Never Trust

Trust is a liability. The moment you trust something — a user, a device, a network segment, a certificate — you have given up the ability to verify it. Zero trust says: do not trust anything by default. Everything must be verified.

This does not mean you trust nothing at all. It means trust is earned through verification, and it is re-earned continuously. A user who authenticates correctly today is not automatically trusted tomorrow. Every request is verified independently.

Principle 2: Always Verify

Verification is the opposite of trust. To verify is to check, every time, without exception. Verify the user. Verify the device. Verify the application. Verify the request. Verify the location. Verify the time of day. Verify the sensitivity of the data being requested.

The standard is: verify explicitly. Use all available data — who the user is, what device they are on, where they are, what they are trying to do, and what the data they want is worth. Make a decision based on all of it, not based on any single factor.

Principle 3: Assume Breach

Traditional security assumes the attacker is outside the perimeter. Zero trust assumes the attacker may already be inside. This is not pessimism. It is realism. The Target breach and the SolarWinds attack both involved attackers who were inside the perimeter for months before they were detected. If you assume the attacker is outside, you will not see the one who is already inside.

Assuming breach changes how you design everything. You encrypt internally, because the attacker may be watching internal traffic. You segment the network, because the attacker may be moving laterally. You monitor everything, because the attacker may be hiding in your logs.

Principle 4: Least Privilege, Applied to Every Request

You studied least privilege in Lesson 3. Zero trust is least privilege applied at the granularity of a single request. A user is not given a role and then trusted for a session. Each request is evaluated independently: is this specific user, on this specific device, at this specific time, allowed to access this specific resource?

If the answer is no, the request is denied. If the context changes — the user moves to a new location, the device fails a security check, the time of day is unusual — the next request is re-evaluated, and it may be denied even if the previous one was allowed.

Principle 5: Protect Every Resource

In the old model, the resources on the inside of the wall were protected by the wall. In zero trust, there is no wall. Every resource — every application, every database, every file, every server — is protected individually. Nothing is safe by virtue of being “inside.”

This is why zero trust networks are sometimes called “microperimeters.” Instead of one big perimeter, you have thousands of tiny ones, each protecting a single resource.

Part 6 — The Five Pillars of Zero Trust Implementation

Principles are intentions. Pillars are how you build them. A real zero trust implementation has five pillars, and each one addresses a different part of the trust problem.

Pillar 1: Identity

Identity is the first pillar, and it is the most important. In zero trust, identity is not just a username and password. It is a verified, continuously assessed assertion of who someone is.

What strong identity looks like:

Multi-factor authentication (MFA) for every user, every time.
Passwordless authentication where possible — passkeys, biometrics, hardware tokens.
Just-in-time and just-enough access, so that a user has only what they need for their role.
Continuous authentication, not just at login. The system re-checks identity throughout throughout the session.
Identity governance: who has what access, and is it still justified?

The problem this solves: a stolen password. In a trust-based network, a stolen password is a key. In zero trust, a stolen password is a failed authentication attempt, because the attacker does not have the second factor.

Pillar 2: Devices

Every device that accesses resources must be verified. Not just the user. The device itself.

What device verification looks like:

Device registration: every device is known and inventory-listed.
Compliance checks: is the device patched? Is it encrypted? Is antivirus running? Is the screen locked?
Certificate-based authentication: each device has a cryptographic identity that cannot be forged.
Continuous monitoring: if a device’s security posture changes mid-session, access is revoked.

The problem this solves: a compromised laptop. An attacker who steals a laptop does not automatically inherit its access, because the device itself must prove it is still the same device, and that it is still secure.

Pillar 3: Network

The network is not a place of trust. It is a collection of connections, and every connection is verified.

What zero trust networking looks like:

Microsegmentation: the network is divided into tiny segments, each protecting a single resource. A compromise in one segment does not spread.
Software-defined perimeters: access is granted per-session, per-user, per-resource. There is no permanent network path that an attacker can exploit.
No implicit trust of internal traffic: traffic inside the network is inspected and authenticated just like traffic from the internet.
Encrypted everything: internal traffic is encrypted, because the attacker may be watching.

The problem this solves: lateral movement. In a traditional network, once the attacker is inside, they can move freely. In zero trust, every hop requires verification, so lateral movement is extremely difficult.

Pillar 4: Applications

Applications are not trusted by default. Every application request is evaluated.

What this looks like:

API gateways that authenticate and authorize every API call.
Service-to-service authentication using mutual TLS (mTLS), so that two services can prove their identities to each other.
Input validation and application-level controls, because the application is the last line of defence.
No trusted internal services: even a database trusts nothing until the request is verified.

The problem this solves: the SolarWinds attack. In that attack, a compromised software update was trusted by every application that installed it. In zero trust, the update would have been treated as a new, untrusted request, and its access would have been limited.

Pillar 5: Data

Data is protected regardless of where it is. Location is not a control.

What this looks like:

Encryption at rest and in transit, everywhere.
Data classification: sensitive data gets stronger controls.
Data loss prevention (DLP): technology that prevents sensitive data from leaving the organisation through email, USB, or cloud upload.
Access controls based on the data’s sensitivity, not on the network the user is on.

The problem this solves: data theft after a perimeter breach. If the attacker gets through every other pillar, the data is still encrypted, and the attacker cannot use it.

Part 8 — Attack and Defence View

Let us now look at zero trust from the attacker’s perspective, because that is how you learn what it is defending against.

How the attacker thinks about trust. The attacker looks for places where trust is assumed. A network where internal traffic is not inspected is a free highway. A service account with a static password is a permanent key. A VPN that gives full network access is a door that stays open.

The classic attacks that zero trust stops:

Credential theft. The attacker steals a password. In a trust-based network, the attacker is inside. In zero trust, the attacker is stopped at the next verification point, because they do not have the second factor.
Lateral movement. The attacker compromises one machine and moves to the next. In a trust-based network, the internal network is open. In zero trust, every hop requires verification, and the attacker is trapped in the segment they already own.
Insider threat. A user with legitimate access does something they should not. Zero trust does not solve this completely, but it limits the damage: the user can only reach the resources their role allows, and every access event is logged.
Supply chain compromise. A software update or a third-party tool is compromised. In zero trust, the update is treated as a new, untrusted entity, and its access is limited by default.

The most important sentence in this lesson: Zero trust does not assume the attacker is outside. It assumes the attacker is already inside, and it designs accordingly.

Part 9 — Common Mistakes Beginners Make

Mistake 1: Thinking zero trust is a product you can buy. You cannot. Zero trust is an architecture and a philosophy. You buy components — identity providers, network segmentation tools, policy engines — but the trust model is a design decision. A company that buys a “zero trust product” and does not change its policies has bought a label, not a security model.

Mistake 2: Confusing zero trust with VPN replacement. Zero trust often replaces VPNs, but it is not the same thing. A VPN gives a user access to a network. Zero trust gives a user access to specific applications, one request at a time. If you replace your VPN with a zero trust proxy but still give users full network access, you have not implemented zero trust. You have moved the perimeter.

Mistake 3: Believing zero trust is only for large organisations. Small organisations can practice zero trust too. MFA on every account, unique passwords, no internal network trust, encrypted everything, and least privilege on access. You do not need a billion-dollar budget. You need the discipline to never assume trust.

Mistake 4: Treating it as a one-time project. Zero trust is a journey, not a destination. Identity verification gets stronger. Devices get more monitored. Networks get more segmented. Data gets better protected. The day you declare “we are zero trust” is the day you stop improving. The attackers are improving too.

Mistake 5: Ignoring the user experience. Zero trust can make life harder for users, and if it does, they will work around it. The goal is security without inconvenience. Single sign-on, passwordless authentication, and well-designed access flows make zero trust usable. If your implementation makes users angry, they will find a way to bypass it, and the model fails.

Part 10 — Guided Mini-Lab: Apply Zero Trust Thinking to Your Own Life

Let us now apply zero trust to your own digital life. This takes 15 minutes.

Step 1: Identify your resources.
What are the things you want to protect? Your email. Your photos. Your bank account. Your private messages. These are your resources, and in zero trust, each one is protected individually.

Step 2: Identify your access points.
How do you reach these resources? Your phone. Your laptop. Your browser. Each one is a device that must be verified.

Step 3: Check your trust assumptions.
Ask yourself: where am I assuming trust?

Do you trust any network by default? (Public Wi-Fi is a classic example. You should not.)
Do you trust any device by default? (A shared computer, a friend’s phone, a library terminal.)
Do you trust any site by default? (A site that asks for your password, but does not have HTTPS.)
Do you trust any email by default? (An email from “your bank” that asks you to click a link.)

Step 4: Apply the zero trust rules.

Never trust: treat every network as hostile. Use a VPN on public Wi-Fi.
Always verify: check the URL before you type a password. Check that the site uses HTTPS. Check that an email is really from the person it claims to be.
Assume breach: if something goes wrong, assume your data is compromised and act accordingly — change passwords, check login history, enable alerts.
Least privilege: do not give apps access to things they do not need. Do not use your email as the admin for everything. Do not share accounts.

Part 11 — Why This Matters for the Rest of Module 2

Let me say something plainly.

Zero trust is the logical endpoint of the principles you have been studying. Least privilege gives you the idea: give people only what they need. Defence in depth gives you the structure: build many layers. Zero trust applies both ideas to every single request, everywhere, all the time. It is the principle you get when you take least privilege and defence in depth and refuse to stop applying them.

The remaining lessons — secure defaults, separation of duties, and case studies — all fit inside the zero trust framework. Secure defaults are how you start with zero trust instead of requiring the user to configure it. Separation of duties is how you prevent a single trusted person from doing something dangerous. The case studies are the proof that the old model fails and the new one works.

If you understand zero trust, you can look at any system and immediately see where it assumes trust, and why that is dangerous. That is the skill this lesson is building.

Part 12 — Summary and Glossary

Here is what we covered.

Zero trust is the principle of never trusting and always verifying. No user, no device, no application, and no network request is trusted by default. The old “trust but verify” model assumed that anything inside the perimeter was safe. It failed because the perimeter disappeared, the attacker got inside, and credentials were stolen.

The core principles are: never trust, always verify, assume breach, least privilege applied to every request, and protect every resource individually.

The five pillars of implementation are identity (MFA, passwordless, continuous verification), devices (registration, compliance, certificates), network (microsegmentation, software-defined perimeters, encrypted internal traffic), applications (API gateways, service-to-service authentication), and data (encryption, classification, DLP).

Zero trust does not assume the attacker is outside. It assumes the attacker is already inside, and it designs accordingly. It is a journey, not a product, and it is not limited to large organisations.

Glossary of 8 terms:

Zero trust — the principle of never trusting and always verifying.
Trust but verify — the old model: trust users inside the perimeter, verify at the boundary.
Microsegmentation — dividing the network into tiny segments, each protecting a single resource.
Software-defined perimeter — access granted per-session, per-user, per-resource, with no permanent network paths.
Passwordless authentication — authentication that does not use passwords: passkeys, biometrics, tokens.
Identity provider — a service that authenticates users and provides identity information to applications.
Compliance check — a verification that a device meets security requirements before it is allowed to connect.
Assume breach — the design assumption that the attacker may already be inside the network.
