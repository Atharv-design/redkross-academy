Module 3: Threat Landscape
Lesson 1: Threat Actor Types — Motives and Capabilities
Part 1 — Hook

A teenager sits in a dark room. She downloads a tool she found on GitHub. She points it at a website and presses enter. The website slows down, then stops responding. She has never written a line of code in her life. She does not know who owns the website. She does not care. She is doing it for the thrill, and because the tool made it easy.

On another continent, a man in a uniform sits in a secure facility. His screen shows a map of the internet. He has a list of targets — power plants, water treatment facilities, government networks. He has spent months preparing. He knows the exact version of the software each target runs. He has a custom exploit that nobody else has. He is not doing this for fun. He is doing it because his government told him to.

In a third place, a group of people meet in a chat room. They are angry about a company’s environmental policies. They decide to deface the company’s website, steal its internal emails, and publish them. They are not professionals. Some of them have technical skills. Some of them just know how to use the tools the others built. They are doing it to make a point, and they want the world to see it.

Three different people. Three different motives. Three different levels of skill. But they are all the same thing to a defender: a threat actor.

This lesson is about learning to tell them apart. If you know who is attacking you, you can predict what they will do, how hard they will try, and what defences will actually stop them. If you treat every attacker the same, you will spend your budget on the wrong defences, and the ones who matter will walk right through.

Part 2 — Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

Explain the motive, capability, and typical targets of each type.
Distinguish between opportunistic and targeted attacks.
Identify which threat actor is most likely to target a given organisation.
Connect threat actor types to the principles of Module 2.

Part 4 — Concept from Scratch

Here is the central sentence of this lesson.

A threat actor is not a mystery. They are a person or a group with a specific motive, a specific level of capability, and a specific set of targets — and if you understand those three things, you can predict their behaviour.

Let me say that again, because it is the foundation of threat intelligence.

A threat actor is not a mystery. They are a person or a group with a specific motive, a specific level of capability, and a specific set of targets — and if you understand those three things, you can predict their behaviour.

The mistake beginners make is lumping all attackers together as “hackers.” That word means nothing useful. A teenager with a downloaded tool is a hacker. A nation-state unit with a million-dollar budget is a hacker. The defences that stop the teenager will not even slow down the nation-state. The defences that stop the nation-state are too expensive for most organisations, and they are overkill for the teenager.

The solution is categorisation. The cybersecurity industry has settled on five main categories of threat actors. They are not perfect — actors evolve, they overlap, and they sometimes change categories — but they are useful enough to be the standard framework. Learn these five, and you will have the vocabulary to talk about threats professionally.

Part 5 — How It Works: The Five Threat Actor Types, One by One
Type 1: Script Kiddies — The Opportunistic Beginners

Who they are. Script kiddies are beginners. They do not write their own code. They do not understand how the exploits work. They download tools that other people wrote, they point them at targets, and they hope something breaks. The name comes from “script” — a pre-written piece of code — and “kiddie” — a dismissive term for someone young and inexperienced.

Motive. Curiosity, boredom, the thrill of seeing something break, and the desire for status in online communities. They are not usually motivated by money, ideology, or espionage. They attack because they can.

Capability. Low. They use automated scanners, public exploit code, and off-the-shelf malware. They do not customise their attacks. They do not persist. If a target is not immediately vulnerable, they move on to the next one.

Typical targets. Anything with an internet connection. Websites with known vulnerabilities. Servers with default passwords. Unpatched systems. They do not choose their targets carefully. They scan broadly and attack whatever responds.

Typical tools. Public exploit databases (Exploit-DB, Metasploit modules), automated scanners (Nmap, Masscan), DDoS booters, credential stuffing lists, phishing kits.

Duration. Hours to days. They have no long-term plan. They attack, see what happens, and stop.

Real example. The Mirai botnet from Module 1 was largely built and operated by script kiddies. The code was released publicly. Thousands of people downloaded it, pointed it at cameras and routers, and built their own botnets. They did not understand the code. They just ran it.

How to stop them. Script kiddies are the easiest to stop. They only succeed against the basics: unpatched software, default passwords, missing firewalls, missing MFA. If you have applied the secure defaults and least privilege from Module 2, script kiddies will almost never succeed against you. They are noise, and good hygiene filters them out.

The mistake beginners make: ignoring them because they are “just kids.” Script kiddies are the canary in the coal mine. If a script kiddie can breach you, a professional will walk through the same door without even noticing it was locked.

Type 2: Cyber Criminals — The Professionals

Who they are. Cyber criminals are professionals. They do this for a living. They have jobs, they have bosses, they have quarterly targets, and they have supply chains. The ransomware groups you read about — LockBit, BlackCat, Conti — are businesses. They have developers who write the malware, negotiators who talk to victims, affiliates who do the initial access, and money launderers who clean the cryptocurrency.

Motive. Money. Pure and simple. They want to steal something they can sell — data, access, computing power — or they want to extort money directly through ransomware, business email compromise, or fraud.

Capability. Medium to high. They use custom tools, but they also buy and sell tools on underground markets. They have access to zero-day exploits, but they prefer to use known vulnerabilities because it is cheaper. They are patient. They will spend weeks doing reconnaissance before they launch an attack. They have persistence: if one path fails, they try another.

Typical targets. Any organisation that has money or data that can be monetised. Hospitals (ransomware pays fast because lives are at stake). Retailers (credit card data sells well). Financial institutions (direct theft). Manufacturing (intellectual property). Small businesses (they pay ransoms because they have no backups). The target is chosen based on return on investment.

Typical tools. Custom malware, ransomware-as-a-service platforms, initial access brokers (who sell compromised credentials), phishing infrastructure, exploit kits, money laundering services.

Duration. Weeks to months for a single campaign. Years for the organisation as a whole. A ransomware group operates continuously, launching new campaigns every week.

Real example. The Colonial Pipeline attack from Module 2 was carried out by a cyber criminal group called DarkSide. They operated as a ransomware-as-a-service business. The affiliates did the initial access (through a weak VPN password). DarkSide provided the ransomware and the negotiation infrastructure. The ransom was paid in cryptocurrency, and DarkSide took their cut.

How to stop them. Cyber criminals are rational actors. They calculate cost versus reward. If attacking you costs more than the expected payout, they will move to an easier target. This means: patch your internet-facing systems (eliminates the cheap exploits), use MFA everywhere (eliminates credential theft), segment your network (limits the blast radius), and have tested backups (removes the leverage of ransomware). If you are a hard target, they will go to the hospital down the street that has none of these things.

The key insight: cyber criminals are not trying to beat your defences. They are trying to find a target where the defences are missing. Make sure you are not that target.

Type 3: Hacktivists — The Ideological Attackers

Who they are. Hacktivists are motivated by ideology, not money. They attack organisations they believe are doing something wrong — environmental destruction, government corruption, human rights abuses, animal testing, censorship. They want to embarrass the target, expose secrets, disrupt services, and send a message.

Motive. Belief. They want to change something, or they want to punish someone. They are often loosely organised — a collective rather than a hierarchy. Anonymous is the most famous example. They recruit publicly. They operate in the open, often announcing their targets in advance.

Capability. Low to medium. Most hacktivists use the same tools as script kiddies — DDoS, web defacement, credential leaks. The more skilled ones can do SQL injection, phishing, and basic network intrusion. They rarely have zero-days or custom malware. But they have something criminals and script kiddies do not: they are willing to be public. They want the attention.

Typical targets. Organisations that are controversial. Governments, oil companies, pharmaceutical companies, political parties, religious organisations, universities, media outlets. The target is chosen for symbolic value, not financial value.

Typical tools. DDoS tools (LOIC, HOIC), web defacement scripts, credential dumping tools, social media for amplification, public paste sites for data leaks.

Duration. Days to weeks for a single campaign. The group may persist for years, but individual campaigns are short and loud.

Real example. In 2011, the hacktivist group LulzSec attacked Sony Pictures, PBS, and the CIA. They defaced websites, stole and published internal data, and mocked their targets on Twitter. They were not trying to make money. They were doing it for “lulz” — amusement — and to prove that the targets were insecure. Several members were eventually arrested.

How to stop them. Hacktivists are predictable. They announce their targets. They attack the public face — the website, the social media, the public-facing services. If you know you are a potential target (because of your industry, your politics, or your public statements), you can prepare: DDoS protection, web application firewalls, monitoring for credential leaks, and a crisis communication plan. The technical defences are the same as for script kiddies, but the preparation is different because you know they are coming.

The key insight: hacktivists want an audience. If you deny them the audience — if your website stays up, your data does not leak, and you respond calmly — they lose. They are not persistent. If the attack fails publicly, they usually move on.

Type 4: Insiders — The Trusted Betrayers

Who they are. Insiders are people who already have legitimate access to your systems. They are employees, contractors, partners, or former employees whose access was not revoked. They do not need to break in. They already have the keys.

Motive. Three main categories:

Malicious insider: greed, revenge, ideology, or coercion. They want to steal data, sabotage systems, or sell access.
Negligent insider: carelessness, ignorance, or bypassing controls for convenience. They click phishing links, send sensitive data to the wrong person, use weak passwords, and ignore policies.
Compromised insider: their credentials have been stolen by an external attacker. They are a victim, not a perpetrator, but from the defender’s perspective, they look like an insider threat.

Capability. High, because they start inside. They do not need to bypass the perimeter. They know the systems, they know the data, they know the processes, and they know the people. A malicious insider with privileged access can do more damage in one day than a nation-state can do in six months.

Typical targets. Their own organisation. The data they have access to: customer databases, source code, financial records, intellectual property, executive communications. The target is chosen by access, not by value — they steal what they can reach.

Typical tools. Their legitimate access. USB drives, personal email, cloud storage, screenshots, printouts. They do not need malware. They use the tools they already have.

Duration. Months to years. A malicious insider often operates for a long time before being detected. The median time to detect an insider threat is over 200 days.

Real example. The payroll fraud from Module 2 Lesson 7 was an insider threat. The employee had legitimate access to the entire financial loop. She used that access to steal 40 lakh rupees over three years. No malware. No exploit. Just access and time.

How to stop them. Insider threats are the hardest to stop, because the attacker is already inside the defences. The answer is not technical. It is organisational:

Separation of duties (Module 2 Lesson 7): no single person controls a complete transaction.
Least privilege (Module 2 Lesson 3): give people only the access they need.
Monitoring and logging: audit what people actually do with their access.
Data loss prevention: prevent sensitive data from leaving the organisation.
Offboarding process: revoke access immediately when someone leaves.
Culture: a culture where people report suspicious behaviour without fear.

The key insight: you cannot prevent insider threats with firewalls. You prevent them with processes, culture, and the discipline to ensure that no single person has unchecked power.

Type 5: Nation-States — The Ultimate Adversary

Who they are. Nation-states are government-sponsored groups. They have the resources of a country behind them. They have intelligence agencies, military units, and dedicated cyber commands. The most famous are APT (Advanced Persistent Threat) groups: APT28 (Russia), APT41 (China), Lazarus Group (North Korea), Equation Group (USA). They are not criminals. They are soldiers and spies.

Motive. Strategic advantage. Espionage (stealing secrets), influence (manipulating elections, shaping narratives), sabotage (destroying infrastructure), and deterrence (showing capability). They are not motivated by money, though some nation-state groups (like Lazarus) also do criminal hacking to fund their operations.

Capability. The highest. They develop their own zero-day exploits, which cost millions of dollars and months of research. They have custom malware that is not detected by any antivirus. They have supply chain access. They can compromise hardware before it ships. They have human intelligence sources inside target organisations. They have the patience to spend years on a single target.

Typical targets. Governments, military, defence contractors, critical infrastructure (power, water, transport), telecommunications, technology companies, political organisations, journalists, dissidents. The target is chosen for strategic value.

Typical tools. Custom zero-day exploits, supply chain compromise, hardware implants, advanced persistent malware, living-off-the-land techniques (using legitimate tools to avoid detection), human intelligence.

Duration. Years. A nation-state operation is measured in years. They compromise a target, establish persistence, and stay there, quietly collecting intelligence, until they are discovered or until they achieve their objective.

Real example. The Stuxnet attack from Module 1 was a nation-state operation (widely attributed to the US and Israel). It targeted Iran’s nuclear enrichment facility. It used multiple zero-day exploits, a stolen digital certificate, and sophisticated knowledge of industrial control systems. It took years to develop and operated undetected for over a year.

The SolarWinds attack from Module 2 was also a nation-state operation (attributed to Russia). It compromised the software supply chain to reach 18,000 customers, including US government departments. It was discovered because a security company noticed an anomaly in its own systems — not because the attack was noisy.

How to stop them. This is the hard truth: most organisations cannot stop a determined nation-state attacker. If a nation-state wants you specifically, and they are willing to spend the resources, they will eventually find a way in.

But you can make it expensive. You can detect them faster. You can limit the damage. The defences that work against nation-states are the same principles from Module 2, applied with extreme rigour:

Zero trust (Lesson 5): assume the attacker is already inside. Verify every request.
Least privilege (Lesson 3): limit what every account can reach.
Defence in depth (Lesson 4): multiple layers, so that one compromise does not give everything.
Separation of duties (Lesson 7): no single person controls a critical system.
Threat hunting (proactive search for attackers): do not wait for alerts.
Supply chain security: verify your vendors, your software, and your hardware.

The key insight: nation-states are not a threat for most organisations. A small business will not be targeted by a nation-state unless it is a supplier to a strategic target. But the defences that stop nation-states are the same defences that stop everyone else, just applied more thoroughly. If you build your security to resist a nation-state, you will automatically stop criminals, hacktivists, and script kiddies.

Part 7 — Opportunistic vs Targeted Attacks

This distinction cuts across the five types, and it is the most practical way to think about defence.

Opportunistic attacks are “spray and pray.” The attacker does not care who the victim is. They scan the entire internet for a specific vulnerability, and they attack everyone who has it. Script kiddies are almost always opportunistic. Cyber criminals are often opportunistic — they use automated tools to find vulnerable servers, then they decide whether the victim is worth pursuing.

Targeted attacks are “sniper shots.” The attacker chooses a specific victim and spends time, money, and skill to compromise them. Nation-states are always targeted. Insiders are by definition targeted (they target their own organisation). Hacktivists are targeted (they choose symbolic victims). Cyber criminals can be targeted — this is called “big game hunting,” where they research a large organisation, find a way in, and deploy ransomware specifically tailored to that victim.

Why the distinction matters. Opportunistic attacks are stopped by hygiene: patching, MFA, secure defaults. If you do the basics, you are invisible to opportunistic attackers. Targeted attacks are stopped by depth: zero trust, segmentation, monitoring, threat hunting. If you are a targeted victim, hygiene is not enough — the attacker will find the one thing you missed.

The practical rule: every organisation must do the hygiene. It stops the noise. Then, assess whether you are a likely target for targeted attacks. If you are (government, defence, critical infrastructure, high-profile, controversial), you must invest in depth. If you are not (small business, low profile), hygiene plus basic depth is usually enough.

Part 8 — Attack and Defence View

Let us now look at threat actors from the defender’s perspective, because that is how you build your defences.

The defender’s mistake: building defences for the wrong actor. A small business buys an expensive threat intelligence platform to stop nation-states, but they do not have MFA on their email, so a cyber criminal steals their payroll data. A hospital invests in advanced threat hunting, but they have a flat network, so a ransomware affiliate spreads to every system in an hour.

The correct approach: match your defences to your threat model.

Identify your likely attackers. Based on your industry, your data, your profile, and your geography, which of the five types are most likely to target you?
Prioritise the defences that stop them. If script kiddies and cyber criminals are your main threat, prioritise hygiene: patching, MFA, secure defaults, backups. If you are a likely target for nation-states or insiders, add depth: zero trust, segmentation, monitoring, separation of duties, threat hunting.
Do not skip the basics. No matter who your likely attacker is, the basics stop the opportunistic attacks that are the background noise of the internet. If you do not do the basics, you are not a target — you are a victim waiting to happen.

The most important sentence in this lesson: You do not defend against “hackers.” You defend against a specific threat actor with a specific motive and capability. Know who you are defending against, or you are defending blindly.

Part 9 — Common Mistakes Beginners Make

Mistake 1: Thinking “it won’t happen to me.” Every organisation with an internet connection is a target for opportunistic attacks. Script kiddies and cyber criminals do not care who you are. They care that you are vulnerable. If you have an unpatched server, a default password, or a missing MFA, you are a target. The only question is when.

Mistake 2: Overestimating the threat. A small business that buys a nation-state-grade threat intelligence feed but does not have backups is spending money on the wrong problem. Match your investment to your actual risk.

Mistake 3: Confusing the actor with the tool. A criminal using a nation-state exploit (like EternalBlue in WannaCry) does not make them a nation-state. The tool is not the actor. The motive, capability, and persistence define the actor.

Mistake 4: Ignoring insiders. Insider threats are the most damaging and the most overlooked. They do not look like attacks. They look like normal work — until the data is gone. The defences are organisational, not just technical.

Mistake 5: Treating threat intelligence as a product. Threat intelligence is not a feed you buy. It is a process: collect data about threats relevant to you, analyse it, and act on it. If you are not acting on it, it is just noise.

Part 10 — Guided Mini-Lab: Build Your Threat Model

Let us now build a simple threat model for your own situation. This takes 15 minutes.

Step 1: Identify your assets.
What do you have that someone might want? Your personal data? Your money? Your accounts? Your reputation? Your devices? List them.

Step 2: Identify your likely attackers.
Based on what you have, which threat actors are most likely to target you?

If you have money and average security: cyber criminals.
If you have a public profile or controversial opinions: hacktivists.
If you work for a strategic organisation: nation-states (indirectly, through supply chain).
Everyone: script kiddies (opportunistic).
If you share devices or accounts: insiders (negligent or compromised).

Step 3: Match the defences.
For each likely attacker, write down the one defence that matters most:

Script kiddies: secure defaults, patching.
Cyber criminals: MFA, backups, segmentation.
Hacktivists: DDoS protection, crisis plan.
Insiders: least privilege, separation of duties, monitoring.
Nation-states: zero trust, threat hunting, supply chain security.

Step 4: Find the gaps.
Which of those defences do you not have? Be honest.

Step 5: Write it down.
Open your lab notebook and write:

LESSON 1 — MY THREAT MODEL
Date: [today]
Assets I protect: [list]
Likely attackers (ranked): [1. most likely, 2. second, ...]
Defences I have: [list]
Defences I am missing: [list]
Top priority fix: [the one missing defence that stops the most likely attacker]

Part 11 — Why This Matters for the Rest of Module 3

Let me say something plainly.

Module 3 is the threat landscape. This lesson gave you the actors. The next lessons will give you their tools, their techniques, and their playbooks. You will learn about malware families, phishing and social engineering, ransomware, DDoS, and how to read a threat report.

But all of those lessons depend on this one. If you do not know who the actor is, you cannot understand why they use that tool, why they choose that technique, and what they are trying to achieve. The malware is not the story. The actor is the story. The malware is just the prop.

If you finish this lesson and you can look at a breach report and say “this was a cyber criminal, not a nation-state, because they used a known vulnerability and demanded ransom,” then you have learned the skill this lesson is for.

Part 12 — Summary and Glossary

Here is what we covered.

There are five main types of threat actors. Script kiddies are beginners using public tools, motivated by curiosity and thrill, stopped by basic hygiene. Cyber criminals are professionals motivated by money, stopped by making yourself a hard target (MFA, backups, segmentation). Hacktivists are ideologically motivated, stopped by DDoS protection and crisis planning. Insiders have legitimate access and are the hardest to stop, stopped by organisational controls (separation of duties, least privilege, monitoring). Nation-states are the ultimate adversary with the highest capability, stopped by extreme rigour in all seven principles of Module 2.

Attacks are either opportunistic (spray and pray, stopped by hygiene) or targeted (sniper shots, stopped by depth). Every organisation faces opportunistic attacks. Only some face targeted attacks. Match your defences to your threat model.

Glossary of 8 terms:

Threat actor — a person or group that poses a threat to an organisation’s assets.
Script kiddie — a beginner who uses pre-made tools without understanding them.
Cyber criminal — a professional attacker motivated by financial gain.
Hacktivist — an attacker motivated by ideology or political belief.
Insider threat — a threat from someone with legitimate access to the organisation.
Nation-state — a government-sponsored group conducting cyber operations for strategic advantage.
Opportunistic attack — an attack that targets any vulnerable system, not a specific victim.
Targeted attack — an attack aimed at a specific, chosen victim.
APT (Advanced Persistent Threat) — a term for sophisticated, long-term threat groups, usually nation-states.
Threat model — an assessment of which actors are likely to target you and what defences are needed.
