Lesson 7: Reading and Writing Threat Reports
Part 1 — Hook

A security analyst sits at her desk at 8:00 AM. A new threat report lands in her inbox from an intelligence provider. It is forty-two pages long. It has charts, graphs, hashes, code snippets, and a title that sounds like a spy novel: “Operation ShadowHammer: APT41 Deploying Novel Mutex-Evasion Techniques across Southeast Asian Logistics Networks.”

She has twenty minutes before her morning briefing with the Chief Information Security Officer. The CISO does not care about mutex evasion. The CISO cares about one thing: are we exposed, and how much is this going to cost us?

Meanwhile, down in the security operations center, the lead engineer is looking at the same report. He does not care about the CISO’s budget. He wants the SHA256 hashes, the command-and-control IP addresses, and the specific firewall rules needed to block the attack before the shift ends.

One report. Two completely different audiences. If the analyst cannot read the report, pull out what matters, and translate it into two different languages — business risk for the executive, technical indicators for the engineer — the report is just expensive PDF art.

This lesson is about that translation. Threat intelligence is only as good as the reports that carry it. If you can read a threat report, you can see what is coming. If you can write a threat report, you can protect an entire organisation.

Part 2 — Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

Define what a threat report is and why it is the primary deliverable of threat intelligence.
Explain the standard anatomy of a professional threat report, from executive summary to technical appendices.
Read a complex threat report and extract actionable intelligence in under ten minutes.
Write a threat report tailored to two distinct audiences: business executives and technical engineers.
Identify and classify Indicators of Compromise (IOCs) correctly and safely.
Avoid common pitfalls in threat reporting, such as burying the lead and including unverified intelligence.
Part 3 — Prerequisites

You have finished the first six lessons of Module 3. You know threat actor types, malware families, phishing, ransomware, DDoS, and insider threats. You also know the core principles from Module 2. This lesson is where all of that knowledge is packaged into written intelligence.

Part 4 — Concept from Scratch

Here is the central sentence of this lesson.

A threat report is not a news article about a hack; it is a structured, evidence-based document designed to answer two questions for two different people: ‘Do I need to worry about my business?’ and ‘What files do I need to block on my firewalls?’

Let me say that again, because it is the definition that separates amateur security writing from professional intelligence reporting.

A threat report is not a news article about a hack; it is a structured, evidence-based document designed to answer two questions: ‘Do I need to worry about my business?’ and ‘What files do I need to block on my firewalls?’

Most people write threat reports like history essays. They start at the beginning, describe every technical detail in chronological order, and end with a vague conclusion about staying vigilant. That is useless. A threat report is an operational tool. It must be structured so that the reader can find what they need in seconds, whether they are running a multi-billion-dollar company or investigating an alert on a single laptop at midnight.

Here is the structural blueprint of every professional threat report.

Part 5 — How It Works: Reading a Threat Report

When you open a threat report, do not read it from page one to the end like a novel. That is a waste of time. Professional intelligence analysts read reports backward or outward, starting from what matters most to their specific environment.

Here is the four-step reading method used by senior analysts.

Step 1: Read the Executive Summary First

Never start with the technical details. Start with the executive summary. It should be one or two paragraphs at the very top of page one.

What you are looking for:

Who is the threat actor? (Is it a criminal group, a nation-state, or a script kiddie?)
What is their target? (Are they hitting financial institutions, healthcare, manufacturing, or everyone?)
What is the impact? (Data theft, ransomware, operational shutdown, espionage?)
Are we in scope? (Do we use the software or operate in the regions mentioned?)

If the executive summary says the attack targets maritime logistics in East Asia, and your company is a retail bank in London, you can stop reading right there. You file the report for situational awareness and move on with your day. That is not laziness; that is efficiency.

Step 2: Scan the Indicators of Compromise (IOCs)

If your organisation is in scope, your next stop is the appendix: the Indicators of Compromise.

What you are looking for:

File hashes (SHA256, MD5) of the malware.
Command-and-control (C2) IP addresses and domain names.
Registry keys, mutexes, and file paths.

You take these indicators and feed them directly into your security tools — your SIEM, your firewall, your endpoint detection system. You run a quick query: have we seen any of these hashes or IPs on our network in the last 30 days? If the answer is yes, you stop reading the report and you open an incident response ticket immediately. The threat is not theoretical anymore; it is in your building.

Step 3: Review the Mitigation Steps

If your IOC query comes back clean (no matches), you look at the mitigation and remediation section, usually found near the end of the report.

What you are looking for:

What specific patches need to be applied?
What firewall rules need to be updated?
What configuration changes prevent this specific technique?

You hand this list to your engineering and IT teams. You do not ask them to read the forty-page report; you give them the action items. “Apply patch KB503441 block domains X and Y by 5:00 PM.”

Step 4: Read the Technical Analysis (If Needed)

Only after you have checked business impact, run your IOCs, and assigned mitigations do you sit down and read the deep technical analysis in the middle of the report.

What you are looking for:

How does the malware execute?
Which step of the kill chain does it exploit?
What tactics, techniques, and procedures (TTPs) does the actor use?

This is for your long-term understanding. It helps you build detection rules, train your analysts, and understand the adversary’s mindset. But it is not the first thing you read during an active threat.

Part 6 — How It Works: Writing a Threat Report

Now let us flip the perspective. You have investigated an incident, or you have gathered intelligence on a new malware campaign, and you have to write the report.

Here is the golden rule of threat writing: know your audience. You are almost never writing for just one person. You are writing a document that will be read by the CEO, the Chief Information Security Officer, the legal team, the press officer, and the junior SOC analyst working the night shift.

To solve this, professional threat reports use a bifurcated structure: high-level business context at the front, deep technical detail at the back.

IMAGE 2 — Audience split diagram: A bifurcation diagram on a white background showing how a single threat intelligence report splits into two distinct paths. Left branch labeled FOR EXECUTIVES (CISO, Board, Legal): business risk, financial impact, strategic decisions, remediation timeline. Right branch labeled FOR ENGINEERS (SOC Analysts, Incident Responders, IT): SHA256 hashes, YARA rules, IP addresses, firewall configuration, memory artifacts. Clean, minimal, professional, 16:9, high resolution.

Let us look at how to write each section.

Writing the Executive Summary

The executive summary must be written in plain English, with zero technical jargon. If you use words like “DLL sideloading,” “buffer overflow,” or “Cobalt Strike beacon” in the executive summary, you have failed.

Bad executive summary: “APT29 deployed a custom PE executable via DLL sideloading into LSASS memory space, establishing an encrypted HTTPS C2 channel over port 443.” (Translation: completely useless to a board member).

Good executive summary: “A state-sponsored threat group is actively targeting financial institutions in our region using fraudulent emails. Their goal is to steal customer financial data. No internal systems have been compromised, but all employees have been alerted, and our perimeter firewalls have been updated to block their known infrastructure.” (Translation: clear, calm, actionable business risk).

Writing the Technical Analysis

This is where you put your engineering hat on. Here you must be ruthlessly precise. Every technical claim must be backed by evidence.

What to include:

The exact timeline of the attack (UTC timestamps).
The vector of entry (how did they get in?).
The malware behavior (what files did it touch, what registry keys did it create, what network connections did it make?).
The kill chain mapping (which phase of the attack does each observed artifact belong to?).

Use clear headings, bullet points, and code blocks for hashes and configuration snippets. Never write a wall of text for technical readers. They want to skim, verify, and act.

Writing the Indicators of Compromise (IOCs)

An IOC is an artifact observed on a network or operating system that with high confidence indicates a computer intrusion. But writing bad IOCs is worse than writing no IOCs at all.

Rules for writing IOCs:

Always specify the type. Do not just list a string of characters; label it as SHA256, IPv4, Domain, or File Path.
Remove false positives. Never list common infrastructure as an IOC (e.g., 8.8.8.8 Google DNS or microsoft.com). If you list a legitimate domain as an IOC, automated security tools will block it, and your company’s network will break. That is called a “self-inflicted denial of service,” and it is a quick way to lose your job.
Keep them machine-readable. Format IOCs in plain text tables or standard formats like CSV or STIX/TAXII so engineers can ingest them directly into security orchestration tools without manual retyping.
Part 7 — Indicators of Compromise (IOCs) and TTPs

To write a great threat report, you must understand the difference between two terms that beginners always confuse: Indicators of Compromise (IOCs) and Tactics, Techniques, and Procedures (TTPs).

David Bianco created a famous mental model called the Pyramid of Pain, which ranks the types of indicators from easiest for the defender to hardest for the attacker to change.

IMAGE 3 — IOC taxonomy diagram: A conceptual pyramid diagram on a white background, representing the Pyramid of Pain across four horizontal tiers from bottom to top. Bottom tier (widest, green): Hash Values — MD5, SHA256 (Easy for attacker to change). Second tier (blue): IP Addresses & Domain Names — infrastructure (Annoying to change). Third tier (amber): Network/Host Artifacts — registry keys, mutexes, file paths (Costly to change). Top tier (narrowest, red): TTPs (Tactics, Techniques, Procedures) — how the actor operates, behavior patterns (Tough-to-impossible for attacker to change without retraining). Clean, structured layout, 16:9, high resolution.

Let us walk up the pyramid:

Hash Values (Bottom): A cryptographic fingerprint of a file. If the attacker changes a single bit in the file, the hash changes completely. It takes the attacker one second to recompile malware with a different hash. For defenders, hashes are easy to check, but trivial for attackers to evade.
IP Addresses and Domain Names: The servers the attacker uses to host malware or receive stolen data. It takes the attacker a few minutes to spin up a new server on a hosting provider. Annoying for them, easy to block for us.
Network and Host Artifacts: Specific registry keys, weird filenames, unique mutexes, or specific user-agent strings in HTTP requests. These cost the attacker more because they have to modify their tool code or deployment scripts.
TTPs (Top): Tactics, Techniques, and Procedures. This is how the actor behaves. Do they use PowerShell for discovery? Do they perform credential dumping via Mimikatz? Do they use spear phishing with weaponized Word documents? TTPs represent the attacker’s habits and training. An attacker cannot change their TTPs easily because it requires changing their operational playbook and retraining their operators.

Why this matters for threat reports: A threat report that consists only of hashes and IP addresses has a very short shelf life — days or weeks. A threat report that describes TTPs and maps them to the MITRE ATT&CK framework has a long shelf life, because it helps defenders recognize the actor even when they change their malware and infrastructure.

Part 8 — Attack and Defence View

Let us look at threat reporting from the perspective of both sides.

The attacker’s view of threat reports: Sophisticated threat actors read public threat reports about themselves. When a security company publishes a report detailing their infrastructure, their malware hashes, and their C2 domains, the attacker’s first action is simple: they throw away those domains, recompile their malware with new hashes, and spin up new infrastructure. The report burns their current tools. That is why threat intelligence is often called a “perishable commodity.” But what the attacker cannot easily change are their TTPs — their habits. That is why good reports focus on behavior, not just hashes.

The defender’s view of threat reports: For the defender, threat reports are the headlights on a dark road. Without them, you are driving blind, waiting for an alert to tell you that you have already crashed. With them, you can scan your logs, patch your systems, and update your defences before the adversary reaches your door.

The golden rule of threat reporting: Intelligence must be actionable. If a report tells you that bad people are doing bad things somewhere in the world, but gives you no way to check if they are touching your systems and no steps to stop them, it is not intelligence. It is news. Threat intelligence is news with a weapon attached.

Part 9 — Common Mistakes Beginners Make

Mistake 1: Burying the lead. Putting the most important finding on page fourteen in a subsection of an appendix. Executives will not read past page one, and engineers will not read past the IOC table. Put the business impact and the IOCs where people can find them immediately.

Mistake 2: Including unverified indicators. Copying and pasting IOCs from social media or unverified blog posts without testing them or checking context. This leads to blocking legitimate services (like Google DNS or Content Delivery Networks) and breaking your own company’s network.

Mistake 3: Writing for yourself instead of the reader. Using excessive technical jargon in the executive summary, or writing a high-level philosophical essay for engineers who need exact firewall rules. Always match the section to the audience.

Mistake 4: Forgetting the MITRE ATT&CK mapping. Not linking observed behaviors to standard framework identifiers (e.g., T1078 Valid Accounts or T1059 Command and Scripting Interpreter). Using MITRE codes allows automated tools across different security platforms to understand the report instantly.

Mistake 5: Treating the report as a one-time event. Publishing a report and never updating it when new intelligence emerges. Threat intelligence is a living process. As the incident evolves, the report must be updated with new hashes, revised timelines, and adjusted mitigation steps.

Part 10 — Guided Mini-Lab: Deconstruct and Summarize a Threat Report

Let us practice reading and summarizing a threat report. This takes 20 minutes.

Imagine you are the senior analyst at a retail bank. You receive a short threat report snippet from your intelligence provider:

THREAT ALERT: Operation GoldenReceipt
Date: November 6, 2026
Actor: FIN8 (Financial Cybercrime Group)
Target: Retail and point-of-sale systems.
Summary: FIN8 is deploying a new PowerShell-based backdoor delivered via malicious PDF attachments sent to finance department employees. Once executed, the script establishes contact with C2 server 198.51.100.42 and attempts to dump memory credentials.
IOCs: SHA256: e3b0c44298fc1c149afbf4c8996fb92427ae41e4649b934ca495991b7852b855, IP: 198.51.100.42.
Mitigation: Block outbound traffic to 198.51.100.42. Disable PowerShell execution for standard user accounts via Group Policy.

Step 1: Extract the Business Impact (For the CISO).
Write two sentences explaining the risk to the bank, without using technical jargon like SHA256 or PowerShell.

Step 2: Extract the Engineering Actions (For the SOC Lead).
List the exact technical items the engineering team needs to do right now based on the snippet.

Step 3: Evaluate the IOCs.
Are the IOCs in the snippet actionable? How long will they remain effective according to the Pyramid of Pain?

Step 4: Write it down.
Open your lab notebook and write:

LESSON 7 — THREAT REPORT DECONSTRUCTION
Date: [today]
Report Title: Operation GoldenReceipt
Executive Summary (for CISO): [2 sentences]
Engineering Action Items (for SOC): [bullet points]
IOC Action: [what was checked/blocked]
Assessment: [is this opportunistic or targeted, high or low risk]

Part 11 — Why This Matters for the Rest of Your Career

Let me say something plainly.

You can be the best technical engineer in the world, you can know every firewall rule, every encryption algorithm, and every malware family by heart — but if you cannot write a clear threat report or brief an executive, your knowledge stays trapped in your head.

Communication is a security control. A clear report stops a breach before it starts because it allows decision-makers to fund the right tools and engineers to apply the right patches. A confusing report wastes hours of valuable time while an adversary is active on the network.

You are learning to be a voice of clarity in a chaotic field. That is what threat intelligence reporting is. The attackers want noise and confusion. The threat report provides the signal.

Part 12 — Summary and Glossary

Here is what we covered.

A threat report is a structured, evidence-based document designed to answer business risk and technical mitigation questions. Professional reports use a bifurcated structure: executive summary at the front for business leaders, technical analysis and IOCs at the back for engineers.

The Pyramid of Pain ranks IOCs from easiest to change (hashes) to hardest (TTPs). TTPs describe how an actor behaves and provide long-term defensive value, while hashes and IP addresses provide immediate, perishable blocking capability.

Good reports avoid burying the lead, include verified machine-readable IOCs, avoid self-inflicted denial of service from false positives, and map observed behaviors to the MITRE ATT&CK framework.

Glossary of 8 terms:

Threat report — a structured document detailing threat actor activity, malware, and mitigations.
Executive summary — the high-level, non-technical overview at the front of a report.
Indicator of Compromise (IOC) — an artifact that indicates a computer intrusion.
Tactics, Techniques, and Procedures (TTPs) — the behavioral patterns and methods used by a threat actor.
Pyramid of Pain — a conceptual model ranking IOCs by how difficult they are for an attacker to change.
False positive — a legitimate item incorrectly flagged as malicious.
Actionable intelligence — intelligence that includes clear, direct steps for defence or mitigation.
CTI lifecycle — the continuous process of gathering, analysing, and sharing threat intelligence.
