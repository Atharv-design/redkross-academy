Part 1 — Hook: Why This Matters

Imagine you are the chief security officer of a mid-sized healthcare organization. One Tuesday morning, a new piece of malware begins silently spreading across hospitals in Europe. It uses a specific, newly discovered vulnerability in a medical software package that your hospital also runs.

You have two choices.

Choice A: You wait for the malware to hit your network, rely on your local antivirus to detect it, hope your firewall catches the traffic, and react after your systems start locking up.

Choice B: Three hours before the attack reaches your region, your team receives an automated threat intelligence alert. The alert includes the exact cryptographic file hashes of the malware, the malicious domain names it communicates with, and the specific vulnerability it targets. Your security software automatically imports these indicators, blocks the domains at your perimeter, and applies a patch to the vulnerable software before a single malicious packet touches your network.

That is the power of Cyber Threat Intelligence (CTI). It transforms security from a reactive game of whack-a-mole into a proactive, strategic defense.

And where does much of this intelligence come from? Surprisingly, a massive portion is gathered legally from publicly available sources — a discipline known as OSINT (Open Source Intelligence). This lesson is about how we collect, analyze, and apply threat intelligence to know what the enemy is doing before they do it to us.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

Identify Indicators of Compromise (IoCs) and Indicators of Attack (IoAs) using Pyramid of Pain concepts.

Part 4 — Concept from Scratch

Here is the central sentence of this lesson.

Threat intelligence is not just raw data about cyber attacks; it is data that has been collected, processed, and analyzed so that security teams can make informed, timely decisions to defend their systems.

Let us break that sentence down, because there is a crucial difference between data, information, and intelligence.

Data: Raw, unformatted facts. Example: 192.168.1.100 or e3b0c44288fc1c149afbf4c8996fb92427ae41e4649b934ca495991b7852b855. On its own, raw data is noise. It tells you nothing about what is happening or what to do.
Information: Data placed into context. Example: “IP address 192.168.1.100 sent 50,000 HTTP requests to our web server in 10 seconds at 2:00 AM.” Now you know what the data represents, but you still do not know who is doing it or why.
Intelligence: Analyzed information that gives you actionable insight. Example: “IP address 192.168.1.100 belongs to a known exit node of a botnet operated by a financially motivated threat actor currently targeting retail web applications using SQL injection attacks. Blocking this IP range and enabling Web Application Firewall (WAF) rule #402 will prevent the breach.”

Now, where does this intelligence come from? It comes from multiple sources, but the most accessible foundation is OSINT.

Open Source Intelligence (OSINT) is intelligence collected from publicly available sources. “Open source” here does not mean open-source software like Linux; it means information that is legally accessible to the public without breaking into any system or using secret government wiretaps. This includes public websites, search engine caches, WHOIS domain domain registration records, DNS records, public code repositories (GitHub), social media posts, threat feeds, and security blogs.

Part 5 — How It Works: The Four Levels of Threat Intelligence

Not everyone in an organization needs the same kind of threat intelligence. A SOC analyst reviewing alerts needs technical details; a Chief Information Security Officer (CISO) presenting to the Board of Directors needs strategic context. Therefore, CTI is divided into four distinct levels.

+-------------------------------------------------------------------+
| 1. STRATEGIC INTELLIGENCE  (Executive level: High-level trends)   |
+-------------------------------------------------------------------+
| 2. OPERATIONAL INTELLIGENCE (Managerial level: Actor TTPs & Intent)|
+-------------------------------------------------------------------+
| 3. TACTICAL INTELLIGENCE    (Architect level: How attacks work)   |
+-------------------------------------------------------------------+
| 4. TECHNICAL INTELLIGENCE   (Analyst level: Raw IoCs & Hashes)    |
+-------------------------------------------------------------------+
1. Strategic Threat Intelligence
Audience: CISOs, Board of Directors, Executive Leadership.
Format: High-level reports, policy briefs, and industry trend assessments written in plain business language without technical jargon.
Content: Broader geopolitical threats, cyber risks facing specific business sectors, financial impact of emerging ransomware trends, and long-term security investments needed.
Example: “Ransomware attacks against healthcare providers in South Asia increased by 40% this quarter, primarily driven by financial extortion groups exploiting unpatched VPN appliances.”
2. Operational Threat Intelligence
Audience: Security Managers, Incident Response Team Leads, Threat Hunters.
Format: In-depth profiling reports detailing specific threat actor groups.
Content: The motives, capabilities, intent, and TTPs (Tactics, Techniques, and Procedures) of specific adversary groups (like APT28 or LockBit). It answers who is attacking us, why, and when.
Example: “Threat Actor Group ‘APT-41’ is actively targeting regional logistics companies. They typically gain initial access via spear-phishing emails containing malicious ISO image attachments, then deploy custom backdoors during weekend hours.”
3. Tactical Threat Intelligence
Audience: System Administrators, Security Architects, Firewall Engineers.
Format: Technical whitepapers, threat advisories, and framework mappings (like the MITRE ATT&CK framework).
Content: Details on how an attack is conducted — the specific software vulnerabilities being exploited, command-line arguments used by malware, or specific network protocols abused.
Example: “The attackers utilize PowerShell script execution bypassing ExecutionPolicy using the -ExecutionPolicy Bypass flag, then establish persistence via Windows Scheduled Tasks executing every 60 minutes.”
4. Technical Threat Intelligence
Audience: SOC Analysts, Automated Security Tools (SIEM, Firewall, EDR).
Format: Machine-readable feeds (STIX/TAXII format), CSV lists, JSON payloads.
Content: Immediate, short-lived Indicators of Compromise (IoCs) — specific IP addresses, malicious URLs, domain names, and cryptographic file hashes (MD5, SHA-256).
Example: SHA-256: 4a8b9c..., IP: 198.51.100.42, Domain: maldocs-update-download.net.
Part 6 — The Pyramid of Pain: Understanding Indicators

In 2013, security researcher David Bianco introduced a concept that revolutionized how security professionals think about threat intelligence: The Pyramid of Pain.

The Pyramid of Pain illustrates how difficult it is for an attacker to adapt when a defender blocks specific types of indicators.

IMAGE 2 — Pyramid of Pain diagram: A 6-tiered pyramid on a white background. Base tier (bottom, green, labeled EASY FOR DEFENDER / TRIVIAL PAIN FOR ATTACKER): Hash Values (SHA-256, MD5). Second tier (teal): IP Addresses. Third tier (blue): Domain Names. Fourth tier (yellow, labeled MODERATE PAIN): Network & Host Artifacts (User-Agents, Registry Keys). Fifth tier (orange, labeled ANNOYING): Tools (mimikatz, Cobalt Strike). Top tier (apex, red, labeled TOUGH / SEVERE PAIN FOR ATTACKER): TTPs (Tactics, Techniques & Procedures). Clean, professional, 16:9, high resolution.

Let us walk up the pyramid from the bottom to the apex to understand why some intelligence is more valuable than others:

1. Hash Values (Trivial Pain for Attacker)
Examples: MD5, SHA-1, SHA-256 hashes of malware files.
Why it is trivial for the attacker: As we learned in Module 3 Lesson 2, polymorphic malware can change its cryptographic hash instantly by modifying a single byte of code. Blocking a file hash takes seconds for a defender, but the attacker can generate a million new hashes in a minute.
2. IP Addresses (Easy Pain for Attacker)
Examples: 203.0.113.50.
Why it is easy for the attacker: Attackers use vast botnets, proxy networks, and cloud infrastructure. If a defender blocks one IP address, the attacker simply switches to another IP out of their pool of thousands.
3. Domain Names (Simple Pain for Attacker)
Examples: bad-domain-login.com.
Why it causes simple pain: Registering a domain name costs money and takes time. If defenders block a domain name at their DNS servers, the attacker must register a new domain, update their infrastructure, and wait for DNS propagation. It is slightly more annoying, but still manageable for an adversary using automated Domain Generation Algorithms (DGAs).
4. Network and Host Artifacts (Moderate Pain for Attacker)
Examples: Specific HTTP User-Agent strings, custom registry keys, unique URI patterns, or file paths used by the malware.
Why it causes moderate pain: If a defender writes a rule to detect a specific registry key or network header pattern, the attacker must modify their malware source code and recompile it to bypass the detection.
5. Tools (Annoying Pain for Attacker)
Examples: Commercial penetration testing tools like Cobalt Strike, credential harvesters like Mimikatz, or custom scanners.
Why it is annoying: If a defender discovers how to detect an entire framework or tool, the attacker loses that tool completely. They must either buy/develop a new tool or heavily modify their existing toolset, requiring significant time and financial investment.
6. TTPs — Tactics, Techniques, and Procedures (Severe Pain for Attacker)
Examples: How the attacker conducts reconnaissance, how they execute lateral movement, or how they exfiltrate data (e.g., using DNS tunneling to steal files).
Why it causes severe pain: TTPs represent the attacker’s habits, training, and operational behavior. Changing a TTP means the attacker must undergo new training, rewrite their operational playbooks, and learn entirely new ways of working. When you detect and block TTPs, you defeat the adversary, not just their tool.
Part 7 — OSINT Techniques and Tools

Now let us explore how defenders (and attackers) collect Open Source Intelligence. Remember: OSINT uses legally available, public information.

1. Passive vs. Active Reconnaissance
Passive Reconnaissance (OSINT): Gathering information about a target without directly sending traffic to the target’s servers or systems. You query third-party databases, search engines, and public archives. The target has no way of knowing you are researching them because your computer never connects to their network.
Active Reconnaissance: Directly probing the target’s systems (e.g., running an Nmap port scan against their public IP addresses, as we practiced in Module 1 Lesson 8). Active recon generates network traffic and log entries on the target’s firewalls.
2. Essential OSINT Data Sources and Tools
Search Engine Hacking (Google Dorks): Using advanced search operators in search engines to uncover exposed files, sensitive configuration documents, or vulnerable login pages indexed by web crawlers.
Example search: filetype:pdf site:example.com "confidential"
Example search: intitle:"index of" "parent directory" passwords
WHOIS & Domain Registries: Public databases that store registration information for domain names, including owner contact details, registration dates, expiration dates, and authoritative DNS name servers.
DNS Enumeration: Inspecting public DNS records (A, AAAA, MX, TXT, CNAME, NS) to discover subdomains, mail servers, and external cloud infrastructure associated with a target domain.
Shodan (shodan.io): Known as the “search engine for internet-connected devices.” Unlike Google, which indexes web page text, Shodan crawls the internet scanning IP addresses and indexing the banners returned by open ports (webcams, industrial control systems, routers, database servers, and IoT devices).
VirusTotal (virustotal.com): A public aggregator that analyzes suspicious files, hashes, domain names, and IP addresses against over 70 commercial antivirus engines and threat intelligence feeds.

Part 8 — Attack and Defence View

Let us examine Threat Intelligence and OSINT through the eyes of both sides.

The Attacker’s Perspective:

Attackers use OSINT during Step 1 of the Cyber Kill Chain (Reconnaissance). Before sending a single phishing email, an attacker will:

Search LinkedIn to map out an organization’s organizational hierarchy, job titles, and employee email formats.
Search GitHub for accidentally committed API keys, passwords, or internal network diagrams pushed by careless developers.
Use Shodan to find unpatched, internet-facing servers running vulnerable software versions.
Search WHOIS and DNS records to find forgotten subdomains or third-party cloud assets that lack strong security controls.
The Defender’s Perspective:

Defenders use OSINT and CTI to conduct Threat Modeling and Defensive Hardening:

Defensive OSINT (Self-Reconnaissance): Perform OSINT against your own organization to discover what an attacker sees. Find exposed API keys on GitHub, shut down forgotten subdomains, and remove sensitive employee details from public websites.
Automated IoC Ingestion: Feed technical threat intelligence (IP blocklists, malicious hashes) directly into SIEMs, Firewalls, and EDR systems to automate detection.
Proactive Threat Hunting: Use tactical and operational intelligence (MITRE ATT&CK mappings) to hunt for adversary TTPs inside the network before alerts fire.
Part 9 — Common Mistakes Beginners Make

Mistake 1: Confusing raw threat feeds with threat intelligence. Subscribing to an uncurated list of 100,000 malicious IP addresses and dumping them into a firewall is not “using threat intelligence.” It leads to massive false positives, degraded firewall performance, and alert fatigue. Intelligence requires context and analysis relevant to your specific organization.

Mistake 2: Crossing the line from passive OSINT to active scanning. Performing WHOIS lookups, checking Shodan, or running Google Dorks is passive OSINT. Running an invasive Nmap vulnerability scan or launching automated exploit tools against a domain you do not own crosses the line into active scanning and can be illegal without explicit written authorization.

Mistake 3: Over-relying on the bottom of the Pyramid of Pain. Many security teams spend all their time collecting and blocking file hashes and IP addresses. Because these indicators are trivial for attackers to change, the defender stays stuck in a reactive loop. Focus effort on detecting artifacts, tools, and TTPs higher up the pyramid.

Mistake 4: Publishing sensitive data during OSINT research. When checking a suspicious file or internal document on public tools like VirusTotal, remember that uploaded files can become publicly downloadable by anyone with an enterprise VirusTotal account. Never upload confidential corporate documents or proprietary source code to public multi-scanners.

Part 10 — Guided Mini-Lab: Performing Passive OSINT Research

Let us perform a safe, completely legal passive OSINT exercise using publicly available web tools. This takes 20 minutes.

Task 1: Perform a WHOIS Lookup
Open your web browser and navigate to a free WHOIS lookup tool (e.g., whois.domaintools.com or whois.com/whois).
Search for a major public domain, such as wikipedia.org.
Inspect the results and answer:
What date was the domain originally registered?
What are the authoritative Name Servers (NS) for this domain?
Is the registrant’s personal address visible, or is it protected by a privacy proxy service?
Task 2: Inspect DNS Records using dig or Web Tools
Open your terminal (or use an online DNS tool like dnschecker.org).
Run a DNS lookup for the MX (Mail Exchange) records of a domain:
bash
   nslookup -type=MX wikipedia.org

(Or on Linux/Mac: dig wikipedia.org MX)
3. Notice how this reveals the third-party email provider or internal mail servers responsible for handling email for that organization.

Task 3: Practice Google Dorking Concepts
Open Google in your browser.
Try this search string (using a generic educational domain or test site):
site:edu filetype:pdf "syllabus"
Notice how Google filters the results to return only PDF documents containing the word “syllabus” hosted on educational .edu domains.
Understand how an attacker uses this exact syntax with terms like filetype:sql or filetype:env to find exposed configuration files containing database passwords.

Part 12 — Summary and Glossary

Here is what we covered.

Cyber Threat Intelligence (CTI) is analyzed threat data that provides actionable insights for security decisions. OSINT (Open Source Intelligence) is intelligence collected legally from publicly available sources. CTI exists at four levels: Strategic (executives/trends), Operational (managerial/actor TTPs), Tactical (architects/how attacks work), and Technical (analysts/raw IoCs).

The Pyramid of Pain illustrates that blocking hashes and IP addresses causes trivial pain to attackers, whereas detecting and blocking TTPs (Tactics, Techniques, and Procedures) inflicts severe pain on adversaries. Passive OSINT gathers information without directly contacting target infrastructure.

Glossary of 10 terms:

Cyber Threat Intelligence (CTI) — processed and analyzed data about cyber threats used to inform security decisions.
OSINT (Open Source Intelligence) — intelligence collected from publicly accessible, legal data sources.
Indicator of Compromise (IoC) — a technical artifact (hash, IP, URL) that indicates a system has been breached.
TTPs (Tactics, Techniques, and Procedures) — the patterns of behavior, tools, and operational methods used by threat actors.
Pyramid of Pain — a conceptual model ranking threat indicators by how much pain blocking them inflicts on an attacker.
Strategic Intelligence — high-level threat intelligence created for executive decision-makers.
Tactical Intelligence — technical intelligence detailing how specific attacks and vulnerabilities operate.
Passive Reconnaissance — gathering intelligence on a target without sending network traffic directly to their systems.
Google Dorking — using advanced search engine operators to find exposed or sensitive public files.
STIX/TAXII — standardized machine-readable formats and protocols used to share threat intelligence automatically.
