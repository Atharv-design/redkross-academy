Lesson 4: Cybersecurity Law, Ethics and Governance
Understanding the Legal and Ethical Framework of Cybersecurity

Security is not just about firewalls and encryption. It is about trust. When an organization collects data, builds systems, or hires people, it enters into a contract of trust with its customers, employees, partners, and society. Laws define the minimum standard of behavior. Ethics define the standard of a professional. Governance is the system that ensures both are followed consistently. A security professional who ignores the law becomes a liability. A security professional who ignores ethics becomes a threat. A security professional who ignores governance creates chaos.

This lesson walks through the legal and ethical landscape that every security professional must navigate. We will explore the moral compass of the profession, the rules of engagement for finding and reporting vulnerabilities, the backbone of Indian cyber law, the new data protection regime, the mandatory incident reporting obligations, the gold standard for security management systems, and the fundamental principles that protect personal data. This is not legal advice. It is the professional knowledge every practitioner must carry.

Professional Ethics and Legal Boundaries

Ethics is not a list of rules you memorize for an exam. It is the internal compass that guides your decisions when no one is watching and the policy manual does not cover the situation. In cybersecurity, you hold keys to the kingdom. You have access to data that can ruin lives, destroy companies, and destabilize governments. Your technical skill gives you power. Ethics is the restraint that ensures you use that power to protect, not to exploit.

The Core Canons of Professional Ethics

The (ISC)² Code of Ethics provides the canonical framework for information security professionals. It consists of four canons, ranked in order of priority. The order matters. When canons conflict, the higher canon prevails.

Canon 1: Protect society, the common good, necessary public trust and confidence, and the infrastructure.
This is the supreme directive. Your primary obligation is to the public, not to your employer, not to your client, not to your career. If your employer asks you to hide a breach that affects millions of users, Canon 1 requires you to resist. If a vulnerability in a medical device could kill patients, Canon 1 demands disclosure even if your employer forbids it. The public trust is the foundation of the profession. Without it, the profession has no legitimacy.

Canon 2: Act honorably, honestly, justly, responsibly, and legally.
This canon governs your personal conduct. Do not lie on your resume. Do not exaggerate your skills. Do not falsify logs. Do not fabricate evidence. Do not take credit for work you did not do. Do not steal code or research. Report your hours accurately. Bill honestly. Disclose conflicts of interest. Obey the law even when breaking it would be convenient. Legal compliance is the floor. Ethical behavior is the ceiling.

Canon 3: Provide diligent and competent service to principals.
Your employer or client deserves your best professional effort. Keep your skills current. Do not sell snake oil. Do not sell services you cannot deliver. Do not overpromise. Communicate risks honestly. If a project is doomed, say so. If a control is ineffective, say so. Competence is an ethical obligation. Incompetence in a security role is negligence.

Canon 4: Advance and protect the profession.
You are a steward of the profession. Mentor junior colleagues. Share knowledge responsibly. Report unethical behavior by peers. Support the development of standards. Volunteer your expertise. Do not bring the profession into disrepute through reckless behavior, public grandstanding, or association with criminal elements.

The ACM Code of Ethics

The Association for Computing Machinery provides a broader ethical framework for all computing professionals. Its principles reinforce the (ISC)² canons with specific guidance for computing professionals.

General Moral Imperatives:
Contribute to society and human well-being. Avoid harm to others. Be honest and trustworthy. Be fair and take action not to discriminate. Honor property rights including copyrights and patents. Give proper credit for intellectual property. Respect the privacy of others. Honor confidentiality.

More Specific Professional Responsibilities:
Strive for excellence in your work. Acquire and maintain professional competence. Know and respect existing laws. Accept and provide appropriate professional review. Give comprehensive and thorough evaluations of computer systems and their impacts, including analysis of possible risks. Honor contracts, agreements, and assigned responsibilities. Improve public understanding of computing and its consequences. Access computing and communication resources only when authorized.

Organizational Leadership Imperatives:
Articulate social responsibilities of organizational units. Manage personnel and resources to enhance the quality of working life. Acknowledge and support proper and authorized uses of computing resources. Ensure that the needs of those affected by a system are clearly articulated. Articulate and support policies that protect the dignity of users and others affected by a computing system. Create opportunities for members of the organization to learn the principles and limitations of computer systems.

Compliance with the Code:
Uphold and promote the principles of the Code. Treat violations of the Code as inconsistent with membership in the ACM.

Legal Boundaries: Where Ethics Meets Law

Ethics sets the higher standard. Law sets the enforceable floor. You must know the legal boundaries of your actions. Ignorance of the law is not a defense in any jurisdiction.

Computer Fraud and Abuse Act (CFAA) — United States
The primary federal anti-hacking law. It criminalizes “unauthorized access” and “exceeding authorized access” to protected computers. The Supreme Court in Van Buren v. United States (2021) narrowed “exceeds authorized access” to mean accessing areas of the computer you are not entitled to access, not merely violating terms of service. However, the law remains broad. Unauthorized scanning, unauthorized access to databases, credential theft, and exceeding the scope of a penetration test engagement can all trigger CFAA liability.

Digital Millennium Copyright Act (DMCA) Section 1201
Prohibits circumventing technological measures that control access to copyrighted works. This affects reverse engineering, security research, and interoperability. The Library of Congress grants limited exemptions every three years for security research, but they are narrow and procedural. Violating DMCA 1201 can result in civil and criminal penalties.

General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) — European Union
Applies to any organization processing personal data of EU residents regardless of location. Requires lawful basis for processing, data minimization, purpose limitation, data subject rights (access, rectification, erasure, portability, objection), data protection by design and by default, data protection impact assessments for high-risk processing, data protection officers for certain organizations, breach notification within 72 hours, and fines up to €20 million or 4% of global turnover.

California Consumer Privacy Act (CCPA/CPRA)
Grants California residents rights to know, delete, opt-out of sale, and non-discrimination. Applies to for-profit entities doing business in California meeting certain thresholds. Requires “Do Not Sell My Personal Information” links, privacy notices, and reasonable security.

India’s Information Technology Act, 2000 (amended 2008)
The primary cyber law in India. Defines cybercrimes (hacking, identity theft, cheating by personation, cyber terrorism, obscenity, child sexual abuse material). Establishes CERT-In as the national incident response agency. Provides for adjudication of cybercrimes and compensation. Amended in 2008 to strengthen provisions on data protection, intermediary liability, and cyber terrorism.

Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 (DPDP Act)
India’s comprehensive data protection law. Establishes rights of Data Principals (individuals) and obligations of Data Fiduciaries (entities processing personal data). Mandates consent, purpose limitation, data minimization, storage limitation, accuracy, security safeguards, breach notification, data protection officers for Significant Data Fiduciaries, Data Protection Impact Assessments for high-risk processing, and cross-border transfer mechanisms. Penalties up to ₹250 crore.

CERT-In Directions (2022)
Mandatory incident reporting within 6 hours. Mandatory clock synchronization with NTP servers (NIC/NPL). 180-day log retention. VPN/Cloud/Crypto KYC. Point of Contact registration. Penalties under Section 70B(7) of IT Act.

Computer Misuse Act (UK), Cybersecurity Law (China), PIPEDA (Canada), LGPD (Brazil), POPIA (South Africa) — Every major jurisdiction has its own framework. If your organization operates globally, you must map the applicable legal landscape.

The Ethics of Vulnerability Research and Disclosure

Finding a vulnerability is not a crime. How you handle it defines your character.

Responsible Disclosure (Coordinated Vulnerability Disclosure)
The industry standard. You discover a vulnerability. You report it privately to the vendor. You give them reasonable time to fix it (typically 90 days). You coordinate the public disclosure with the vendor after a patch is available. You do not publish exploit code before a patch is available. You do not sell the vulnerability to brokers or governments. You do not use it for personal gain.

Full Disclosure
Publishing all details of a vulnerability immediately without vendor coordination. Proponents argue it forces vendors to act and informs users immediately. Critics argue it arms attackers before users can patch. Generally considered irresponsible unless the vendor has been unresponsive for an extended period despite good-faith efforts.

Non-Disclosure
Reporting only to the vendor, never publishing. Used by intelligence agencies and some vendors. Leaves users unaware of risk. Generally considered unethical by the security research community unless national security is at stake.

Bug Bounty Programs
Formalized responsible disclosure with financial rewards. Platforms like HackerOne, Bugcrowd, Intigriti. Provide legal safe harbor, defined scope, reward tiers, and triage processes. Researchers get paid; companies get bugs fixed. The model works when both sides act in good faith.

Legal Safe Harbor
A written authorization from the organization defining what testing is permitted, what is out of scope, what data you may access, and a promise not to pursue legal action for good-faith research within scope. Without a safe harbor, you are exposed to CFAA, DMCA, and IT Act liability. Always get written authorization before testing.

The Ethics of Dual-Use Tools and Knowledge

You write a tool that can be used for penetration testing or for ransomware. You discover a vulnerability that could be used to steal data or to protect users. You train a junior analyst who later uses skills for crime. Knowledge is dual-use. The ethical burden is on you to:

Restrict distribution of offensive capabilities.
Implement safeguards in your tools (authentication, logging, kill switches).
Publish defensive uses and mitigations alongside offensive research.
Refuse to sell capabilities to oppressive regimes or criminal groups.
Support defensive mitigation alongside offensive research.
Responsible Disclosure and Bug Bounty

Finding a vulnerability is a moment of power. What you do next defines your professional character. The industry has converged on Coordinated Vulnerability Disclosure (CVD) as the gold standard. It is a structured process that balances the researcher’s right to publish with the vendor’s need to fix and the public’s need to be protected.

The CVD Lifecycle

1. Discovery and Verification
You find a potential vulnerability. Verify it. Write a minimal, non-destructive proof-of-concept. Document the steps to reproduce, the affected versions, the impact, and the attack vector. Check if it is already known (CVE database, vendor advisories, GitHub issues, Exploit-DB). If it is known, stop. If it is new, proceed.

2. Private Reporting
Find the correct channel. Check the vendor’s security.txt file (/.well-known/security.txt), their security page, their bug bounty platform (HackerOne, Bugcrowd, Intigriti), or email security@. Do not post on Twitter, Reddit, GitHub Issues, or public forums. Your report must include:

Clear title: “CVE-Request: RCE in Product X v1.2.3 via Deserialization”
Affected product and versions.
Step-by-step reproduction steps (scripts, screenshots, curl commands).
Impact assessment (CVSS score, data at risk, exploitability).
Proof-of-concept (safe, non-destructive).
Suggested mitigation (if known).
Your contact information and PGP key.
Requested disclosure timeline (typically 90 days).

3. Vendor Triage and Acknowledgment
The vendor should acknowledge within 1-3 business days. They confirm the vulnerability, assign a severity (CVSS), assign a CVE ID (or request one from a CNA), and start the remediation clock. They should provide a point of contact for updates.

3. Remediation (The Embargo Period)
The vendor develops the fix. The standard embargo is 90 days. If the vulnerability is being actively exploited in the wild, the window shrinks to 7 days (Google Project Zero standard) or even immediate disclosure if the vendor is unresponsive. During this period, you do not disclose publicly. You may share details with other affected vendors under NDA if the vulnerability affects a shared library or protocol. The vendor provides weekly status updates.

4. Coordinated Release
The vendor releases the patch and a security advisory. You publish your detailed write-up (technical details, root cause, exploit mechanics, mitigation). Both parties release simultaneously. The CVE record is updated. The CVE entry links to the vendor advisory and your write-up. You get credit in the advisory.

5. Post-Disclosure
Monitor for exploitation in the wild. Update your advisory if new vectors emerge. The vendor monitors for exploitation and releases follow-up patches if needed. The CVE record is updated. The cycle closes.

Bug Bounty Programs: Formalizing the Exchange

Bug bounty platforms (HackerOne, Bugcrowd, Intigriti, Intigriti, YesWeHack) provide the infrastructure for CVD at scale.

Scope Definition
The program defines what is in scope (domains, APIs, mobile apps, specific versions) and what is out of scope (third-party services, physical security, social engineering, DoS, spam). Clear scope prevents accidental out-of-bounds testing.

Reward Structure (Typical Tiers)

Severity	CVSS Range	Typical Reward Range
Critical	9.0 - 10.0	$10,000 - $100,000+
High	7.0 - 8.9	$3,000 - $15,000
Medium	4.0 - 6.9	$500 - $3,000
Low	0.1 - 3.9	$100 - $500
Informational	0.0	$0 - $100 (Swag/Recognition)

Modifiers: Quality bonus (exceptional report), Chain bonus (exploit chain), First-finder bonus.

Safe Harbor (The Legal Shield)
The program’s terms of service constitute a legal safe harbor. They promise not to pursue legal action (CFAA, DMCA, state laws) for activities within scope conducted in good faith. Requirements: good faith, scope adherence, no data exfiltration, no service disruption, no social engineering, no DoS, no unauthorized access beyond PoC, confidential handling of vulnerability details until coordinated disclosure.

Triage SLAs (Vendor Commitments)
Acknowledgment: < 24 hours (Critical/High), < 7 days (Medium/Low).
Triage/Validation: < 72 hours (Critical/High), < 7 days (Medium/Low).
Resolution: 30 days (Critical), 60 days (High), 90 days (Medium), 180 days (Low).
Payment: Within 30 days of fix verification.

Mediation
Platforms provide mediation for disputes over severity, duplicate status, or reward amount.

The Researcher’s Playbook
Read the Policy — Every word. Scope, exclusions, reward structure, safe harbor terms, disclosure timeline, attribution policy.
Stay In Scope — Test only in-scope assets. Do not test out-of-scope assets “just to check.”
Minimize Impact — Do not access data you don’t need. Do not modify data. Do not disrupt services. Use test accounts. Use test environments if provided.
Document Everything — Screenshots, logs, timestamps, commands, network captures. You need evidence for the report and for your own protection.
Communicate Professionally — Be responsive. Be patient. Be respectful. The triager is a human.
No Extortion — Never threaten public disclosure to force payment or faster fixing. That is extortion.
Respect the Embargo — Do not disclose until the vendor authorizes. Coordinated release is the contract.
Appeal Process — If you disagree with severity or duplicate ruling, use the platform’s mediation process. Do not go public in anger.
IT Act 2000 Overview

The Information Technology Act, 2000 (IT Act) is the primary legislation governing cyber activities in India. Originally enacted in 2000, it was significantly amended in 2008 (effective 2009) to address cybercrime, data protection, electronic signatures, and critical infrastructure protection. It is the backbone of Indian cyber law.

Structure and Scope

The IT Act applies to the whole of India. It has extraterritorial jurisdiction under Section 1(2) read with Section 75: it applies to any offense or contravention committed outside India if it involves a computer, computer system, or computer network located in India. This means an attacker in another country hacking an Indian server is liable under the IT Act.

Key Definitions (Section 2)
Computer — Any electronic, magnetic, optical, or other high-speed data processing device or system performing logical, arithmetic, and memory functions. Includes input, output, processing, storage, software, and communication facilities connected to it.
Computer System — A device or collection of devices including input, output, storage, software, firmware, and communication facilities.
Computer Network — Interconnection of one or more computers through communication media (satellite, microwave, optical fiber, cable, etc.).
Data — Information, knowledge, facts, concepts, instructions in formalized manner processed or being processed in a computer system or network.
Electronic Record — Data generated, sent, received, or stored in electronic form.
Electronic Signature — Authentication of electronic record by subscriber using asymmetric crypto system and hash function (includes digital signature, Aadhaar eSign).
Intermediary — Any person who on behalf of another receives, stores, transmits electronic records or provides services (ISPs, cloud providers, social media, marketplaces, payment gateways).
Cyber Security — Protecting information, equipment, devices, computer, computer resource, communication device, and information stored therein from unauthorized access, use, disclosure, disruption, modification, destruction.
Critical Sections for Cybersecurity Practitioners

Section 43 — Damage to Computer System (Civil Liability)
Covers unauthorized access, downloading/extracting data, introducing viruses/contaminants, damaging systems/networks, disrupting services, denying access, tampering with source code. Provides for compensation up to ₹1 crore payable to the affected party. Adjudicated by an Adjudicating Officer appointed by the Central Government.

Section 65 — Tampering with Computer Source Documents
Knowingly or intentionally concealing, destroying, altering, or causing another to conceal/destroy/alter computer source code when required to be kept by law. Punishment: Up to 3 years imprisonment + fine up to ₹2 lakh.

Section 66 — Computer Related Offenses (Criminal)
Doing any act referred in Section 43 dishonestly or fraudulently. Up to 3 years imprisonment + fine up to ₹5 lakh. This is the primary “hacking” provision. Covers unauthorized access, data theft, malware distribution, system disruption.

Section 66C — Identity Theft
Fraudulently using electronic signature, password, or unique identification feature of another person. Up to 3 years + fine up to ₹1 lakh. Covers credential stuffing, session hijacking, SIM swap, API key theft.

Section 66D — Cheating by Personation Using Computer Resource
Cheating by personation using any communication device or computer resource. Up to 3 years + fine up to ₹1 lakh. Covers BEC, impersonation scams, fake support calls, deepfake video calls.

Section 66E — Violation of Privacy
Capturing, publishing, transmitting images of private area without consent. Up to 3 years + fine up to ₹2 lakh. Covers revenge porn, hidden cameras, spyware camera access.

Section 66F — Cyber Terrorism
Acts with intent to threaten unity, integrity, security, sovereignty of India; deny access to authorized personnel; access protected systems without authorization; introduce contaminants. Punishment: Imprisonment for Life. Covers attacks on critical infrastructure, power grid, banking, telecom, government systems.

Section 43A — Body Corporate Liability for Data Protection
Body corporate possessing, dealing, handling sensitive personal data (SPD) in a computer resource it owns, controls, or operates. If negligent in implementing “reasonable security practices and procedures” causing wrongful loss/gain, liable to pay compensation (up to ₹5 crore). Basis for SPDI Rules 2011 (Reasonable Security Practices).

Section 66F — Cyber Terrorism
Intent to threaten unity/integrity/security/sovereignty of India; deny access; penetrate/access a computer resource without authorization; introduce contaminant. Punishment: Imprisonment for Life. Covers attacks on critical infrastructure (power grid, banking, telecom, govt).

Section 69 — Power to Intercept, Monitor, Decrypt
Central/State Government can direct any agency to intercept, monitor, decrypt information generated, transmitted, received, stored in any computer resource. Grounds: sovereignty, security, defense, friendly relations, public order, preventing cognizable offenses. Requires written order by competent authority.

Section 69A — Blocking of Information
Central Government can direct intermediaries to block public access to any information. Grounds: same as Section 69. Used for website/app blocking (TikTok, PUBG, etc.). Emergency blocking without hearing (48-hour review).

Section 69B — Monitoring and Collection of Traffic Data
Central Government can authorize monitoring/collection of traffic data (metadata) through any computer resource for cyber security. Mandatory technical assistance from intermediaries.

Section 70 — Protected Systems (Critical Information Infrastructure)
Appropriate Government declares any computer resource as a Protected System. Unauthorized access = Up to 10 years + Fine. NCIIPC (National Critical Information Infrastructure Protection Centre) is the nodal agency.

Section 70B — Indian Computer Emergency Response Team (CERT-In)
Establishes CERT-In as the national nodal agency for incident response. Empowers CERT-In to issue directions, collect information, coordinate response. Section 70B(4) & (6) — Basis for CERT-In Directions 2022 (mandatory 6-hour reporting, NTP sync, log retention, VPN KYC, PoC).

Section 79 — Intermediary Exemption (Safe Harbor)
Intermediary not liable for third-party content if: role limited to providing access, no initiation/selection/modification of content, observes due diligence (IT Rules 2021), removes content on court/government order. Loss of safe harbor = liability for user content.

Section 43A — Reasonable Security Practices (Body Corporate Liability)
Body corporate handling Sensitive Personal Data (SPD) negligent in implementing “reasonable security practices and procedures” (SPDI Rules 2011) causing wrongful loss/gain → liable for compensation (up to ₹5 crore). Basis for SPDI Rules 2011 (ISO 27001 as benchmark).

Section 72A — Disclosure of Personal Information in Breach of Contract
Disclosure of personal information in breach of lawful contract. Up to 3 years + ₹5 lakh. Applies to body corporate, employees, processors.

Section 72 — Breach of Confidentiality
Breach of confidentiality by person with access under IT Act powers. Up to 2 years + ₹1 lakh.

Section 67/67A/67B — Content Offenses
67: Obscene material (3 years/5 years). 67A: Sexually explicit material (5/7 years). 67B: Child Sexual Abuse Material (5/7 years, non-bailable). Strict liability for intermediaries if they don’t remove on notice.

Section 69/69A/69B — Government Powers
69: Interception/Monitoring/Decryption (requires Secretary-level order). 69A: Blocking of information (websites, apps). 69B: Monitoring/Collection of Traffic Data (metadata). Mandatory technical assistance from intermediaries.

Section 70 — Protected Systems (Critical Information Infrastructure)
Government declares computer resource as Protected System. Unauthorized access = Up to 10 years + Fine. NCIIPC is nodal agency.

Section 70B — CERT-In (Indian Computer Emergency Response Team)
Nodal agency for incident response. Issues directions under 70B(4)&(6). Basis for CERT-In Directions 2022 (6-hour reporting, NTP sync, 180-day logs, VPN KYC, PoC). Section 70B(7): Penalty — 1 Year Imprisonment + ₹1 Lakh Fine.

Section 79 — Intermediary Exemption (Safe Harbor)
Intermediary not liable for third-party content if: role limited to access/transmission/storage, no initiation/selection/modification, observes due diligence (IT Rules 2021). Loss of safe harbor = liability for user content.

Section 72A — Disclosure of Personal Information in Breach of Contract
Up to 3 years + ₹5 lakh. Applies to body corporate, employees, processors disclosing personal info in breach of contract.

Section 84A/84B/84C — Encryption, Abetment, Attempt
84A: Modes of encryption (Central Govt prescribes). 84B: Abetment of offenses. 84C: Attempt to commit offenses.

DPDP Act 2023 Overview

The Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 (DPDP Act) is India’s comprehensive data protection law. Enacted August 11, 2023. Not yet fully notified (Rules pending). Preparation must begin now.

Scope and Applicability (Section 3)
Applies to processing of Digital Personal Data within India.
Extraterritorial: Applies to processing outside India if it involves offering goods/services to Data Principals within India.
Exemptions: Personal/domestic processing, State instrumentalities (notified), Startups (notified, limited period), Research/Statistical (anonymized).
Core Definitions (Section 2)
Data Principal (DP): Natural person to whom personal data relates (Child → Parent/Guardian; Disabled → Guardian).
Data Fiduciary (DF): Person (natural/legal) who alone or jointly determines purpose and means of processing.
Data Processor: Processes personal data on behalf of Data Fiduciary.
Significant Data Fiduciary (SDF): Notified by Central Govt based on Volume, Sensitivity, Risk, Turnover, Technology. Extra obligations.
Consent Manager: Registered entity providing interoperable platform for consent lifecycle (Give, Review, Withdraw).
Data Protection Officer (DPO): Appointed by SDF. India Resident. Contact Published. Reports to Board.
Data Protection Board (DPB): Adjudicatory Body (Digital Office). Adjudicates complaints, imposes penalties.
Seven Core Principles (Section 4)
Principle	Legal Text	Engineering Translation

# Pseudo-code: Consent Enforcement Middleware
async def enforce_consent(request, purpose_id: str, data_categories: List[str]):
    principal_id = get_principal_id(request)
    
    # 1. Fetch Consent Artefact from Consent Registry (Cache: Redis TTL 5m)
    consent = await consent_registry.get(principal_id, purpose_id)
    
    # 2. Verify Validity
    if not consent:
        raise ConsentRequiredError(f"No consent for purpose {purpose_id}")
    if consent.expires_at < now():
        raise ConsentExpiredError("Consent expired. Re-consent required.")
    if not consent.covers_categories(data_categories):
        raise ConsentScopeError(f"Consent does not cover categories: {data_categories}")
    if consent.withdrawn_at:
        raise ConsentWithdrawnError("Consent withdrawn by user.")
    
    # 4. Log Access (Audit Trail)
    await audit_log.log(DataAccessEvent(
        principal_id=principal_id,
        purpose_id=purpose_id,
        categories=data_categories,
        consent_version=consent.version,
        timestamp=now(),
        actor="system"
    ))
    
    return ConsentContext(consent_id=consent.id, version=consent.version)

Part 15 — Summary and Glossary

Here is what we covered.

The DPDP Act 2023 is India’s comprehensive data protection law. It establishes seven principles (Lawful/Fair/Transparent, Purpose Limitation, Minimization, Accuracy, Storage Limitation, Integrity/Confidentiality, Accountability) that map directly to engineering controls.

The Act defines six roles: Data Principal (Rights Holder), Data Fiduciary (Accountability Anchor), Data Processor (Sub-contractor), Significant Data Fiduciary (Extra Obligations), Consent Manager (Interoperable Platform), Data Protection Officer (Accountability Owner), Data Protection Board (Enforcer).

Consent is the primary lawful basis. It must be Free, Informed, Specific, Unconditional, Unambiguous, Withdrawable, Verifiable. Implemented via Consent Artefacts (Signed JWS) stored in a Consent Registry, enforced by Policy Engine Middleware.

Data Principal Rights (Access, Correction, Erasure, Grievance, Portability, Nomination) must be automated via APIs with SLAs (7-day Grievance, Immediate Erasure Initiation).

DPIA is mandatory for Significant Data Fiduciaries and High-Risk Processing (Special Categories, Large Scale, Profiling, Children, New Tech, Public Monitoring). The DPIA Pipeline (Threshold → Assessment → Mitigation → DPO Sign-Off → Monitor) must be integrated into CI/CD.

Cross-Border Transfers require Adequacy Decision or SCCs/BCRs/Derogations. India has no Adequacy Decision for EU/US yet — SCCs are mandatory.

Significant Data Fiduciaries (SDFs) have extra obligations: Mandatory DPO (India Resident), Mandatory DPIA for All Processing, Annual Audit, Algorithm Transparency, Board Reporting.

Engineering Implementation requires: Data Mapping, Consent Middleware, Rights APIs, DPIA Gates in CI/CD, DPA Automation, Breach Notification Automation, Metrics Dashboards.
