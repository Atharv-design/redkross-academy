## Why the shared medium forces protocol-level defence Context: the defining lesson of wireless security history is that physical control of the medium is impossible, so security must live in the protocol. The collapse of WEP (its encryption was broken, making captured traffic readable), the rise of deauthentication-based attacks against unprotected management frames, and the industry's responses - WPA2 (802.11i, strong encryption), 802.11w (Protected Management Frames), and WPA3 - all follow directly from the broadcast nature of radio. Standards bodies (IEEE, Wi-Fi Alliance) and guidance such as NIST SP 800-153 codify these protocol-level defences precisely because the air cannot be fenced [C16-M01-R1][C16-M01-R2]. Why it matters for this module: it explains why the course's hardening focuses on encryption, PMF and authenticity rather than on "hiding" the network - you cannot hide a broadcast, so you must protect it. Lessons: you cannot physically control the medium; enforce confidentiality, integrity and authenticity in the protocol (strong crypto, PMF, strong auth), and detect impostors.



## (FICTIONAL TEACHING SCENARIO): The SSID anyone can wear Context: at HimalayaCorp (fictional, modelled by the lab data), staff connect to the "HimalayaCorp" Wi-Fi by its name. What happened: an attacker in the car park stood up an access point also advertising "HimalayaCorp" - an evil twin - on an unknown BSSID, open, with a stronger signal near the entrance. To a user, the name looked identical. In the lab this is exactly the observed_aps.json scan: the managed SSID appears on authorised BSSIDs AND on unknown ones. The defender's offline analysis (roguescan.py) flagged the impostor BSSIDs against the authorised inventory. Response: because the organisation maintained a BSSID inventory and reviewed site surveys, the impostor was detected and could be investigated and removed; PMF and strong authentication (Module 2) reduce what such an impostor can achieve, and user awareness helps staff avoid it. Lessons: the SSID is a name anyone can wear; authenticity comes from BSSID inventory, PMF, strong auth and detection - not from the network name.
