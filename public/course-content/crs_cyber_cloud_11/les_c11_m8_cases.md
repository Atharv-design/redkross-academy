## Code Spaces, June 2014 - recovery that lived in the same account ------------------------------------------------------------------------ Context: As reported by The Register on 18 June 2014, Code Spaces said an intruder had gained access to its Amazon EC2 control panel and demanded money. When the company attempted to regain control, the intruder deleted data and backups, and the company said it could no longer operate [C11-M08-R2]. Response and architecture lessons: - Backups must be in a separate account with separate credentials and immutability, so that control-plane access to production cannot delete them (Module 4). - Administrative access to the control plane needs phishing-resistant MFA and few standing privileges (Module 2). - Containment plans must assume the intruder is watching and acting in parallel: break-glass access, pre-tested runbooks and the ability to revoke all sessions quickly matter. - Destructive actions (mass deletion, key deletion) deserve real-time alerts and, where possible, preventive guardrails.



## (FICTIONAL TEACHING SCENARIO): Terminate first, ask later ----------------------------------------------------------------------- This scenario is fictional. During a Tamarind incident an engineer terminated a suspicious instance to "stop the attack". The disk and memory evidence were lost, and the team could not determine what data had been accessed, forcing a broader notification. The runbook now requires isolation, snapshot and tagging before any termination, and termination protection for production instances.
