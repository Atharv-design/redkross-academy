## DigiNotar, 2011 - when a certificate authority falls ---------------------------------------------------------------------------- Context: In 2011 an intruder compromised the Dutch certificate authority DigiNotar. A rogue wildcard certificate for google.com, issued by DigiNotar, was posted publicly on 28 August 2011. The investigation by Fox-IT ("Black Tulip") identified 531 rogue certificates. Audit data showed around 300,000 IP addresses using a rogue google.com certificate in one day, almost all from Iran, consistent with interception of users' traffic. Microsoft, Mozilla and Google removed DigiNotar from their trust stores, and the company went bankrupt later that year [C15-M05-R2]. Lessons: - Any trusted CA can issue for any domain, so the web PKI is only as strong as its weakest trusted CA. - Certificate Transparency, CAA records and CT monitoring were strengthened in the years after incidents like this. They make mis-issuance visible. - CA key and network security are critical. Offline roots, HSMs, segmentation and logging are not optional. - Relying parties need the ability to distrust a CA quickly. Verification note: details are from multiple contemporaneous reports and Fox-IT findings as reported (web-search cross-check).



## (FICTIONAL TEACHING SCENARIO): verify=False in production ------------------------------------------------------------------------- This scenario is fictional. A Tamarind integration script failed with "unable to get local issuer certificate" after the partner changed its certificate. A developer set verify=False to restore service. Months later a review found that the script sent partner credentials to whatever server answered. The real cause was the partner serving the leaf without its intermediate (validation case 2). The fix was to ask the partner to serve the full chain, restore verification, and add a CI check that bans verify=False (a Semgrep rule, Course 12).
