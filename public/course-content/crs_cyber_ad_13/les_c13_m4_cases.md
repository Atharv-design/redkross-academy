## NotPetya and the cost of flat administration, 2017 Context: the NotPetya malware of June 2017 spread destructively through many enterprises. Although its initial spread used specific techniques, a major reason it reached entire estates so fast was the widespread practice of using the SAME local administrator password on many machines and exposing high-privilege domain credentials on ordinary systems - flat administration with no tier containment. Once the malware had administrative credentials valid across many hosts, nothing stopped it. Maersk's widely reported recovery, which required rebuilding much of its Active Directory, became a reference point for why identity containment and recovery planning matter [C13-M04-R5]. Why it matters for this module: the damage was an identity-architecture failure as much as a malware event. Unique local admin passwords (LAPS), tiered administration and protected credentials would each have slowed or contained it. Lessons: flat administration turns any foothold into estate-wide control; LAPS, the tier model and credential protection are containment controls, not optional polish; plan AD recovery before you need it.



## (FICTIONAL TEACHING SCENARIO): The admin who browsed Context: at Sahyadri Logistics (fictional), an administrator uses one account for everything, including domain administration, and logs on to their everyday laptop with it to read email. What happened: a phishing document runs on the laptop. Because the domain-admin account had authenticated there, its credentials were present in memory and were harvested; the attacker reused them to reach a domain controller. A single click became domain compromise. Response and prevention: the firm splits the account into a Tier 2 everyday account and a separate Tier 0 admin account; the Tier 0 account is added to Protected Users (AES-only, no NTLM/delegation, four-hour TGT) and is used only from a privileged-access workstation that cannot browse or read email; local admin passwords are moved to LAPS. The same phishing click now compromises only a Tier 2 laptop. Lessons: separate accounts by tier; never administer Tier 0 from a general-purpose machine; Protected Users and PAWs turn the rule into an enforced control.
